        Multi-Agent 5G Slice Admission

Manoj Prasad Kunasegran, Wai Leong Pang* , Swee King Phang
School of Engineering, Faculty of Innovation and Technology, Taylor’s

                                    Abstract
 Fifth generation (5G) network slicing must admit heterogeneous requests across
 cooperating base stations under strict per-slice guarantees. Multi-agent learn-
 ing can adapt these decisions to changing load, yet learned controllers are rarely
 tested against trivial policies or on independent seeds. We present AXIOM,
 short for Adaptive Cross-cell Integrated Orchestration of Multi-slice admission.
 Its encoder, shared by every gNodeB (gNB), feeds one decision head per slice,
 keeping its size fixed as clusters grow. A pre-registered protocol with both
 checks compares it with single-agent and independent deep Q-networks (DQN),
 multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), value decomposition, and
 tuned rules, under static and coupled time-varying load. At 7, 19, and 37
 gNBs, AXIOM keeps Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) and
 Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) service-level compliance above 93%, while
 every learned baseline stays below 84%. A static rule shedding all machine-type
 requests keeps at least 99.96%, and a tuned rule beats every learner at its tuning
 conditions. When load rises 10% to 20% past them, AXIOM overtakes that rule
 by up to 8.7 reward per step. At 37 gNBs, AXIOM leads the best learned base-
 line by 35.0 to 36.5 points, with a URLLC backlog delay 7 to 44 times shorter
 than the learned baselines and 7,782 parameters against up to 682,058. Under
 coupling, AXIOM earns 27% to 36% more reward than MAPPO at 3 and 19
 gNBs, and 39% more than that static rule at 3 gNBs. AXIOM is therefore a
 viable alternative for scalable 5G slice admission.

 Keywords: Network Slicing, Slice Admission Control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement
 Learning, Graph Neural Networks, Open RAN

1 Introduction
Network slicing virtualises the Radio Access Network (RAN) to serve Ultra-Reliable
Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and
Massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC), each under its own Service Level
Agreement (SLA). Our earlier simulation studies showed that deep reinforcement
learning, including a deep Q-network (DQN), can protect URLLC SLAs at a single
congested gNodeB (gNB) [16] and can balance load across a gNB cluster [17]. How-
ever, those controllers used a centralised approach, whereas a real deployment spans
many gNBs that share spectrum planning and a cluster-wide load-balance objective.
Consequently, admission control across such a cluster becomes a multi-agent problem.
In this research, we ask whether per-gNB agents that share a view of the whole clus-
ter can decide better than a single centralised agent or independent per-gNB agents.
Fig. 1 places our research question within the broader system context.
    This paper introduces AXIOM, an acronym for Adaptive Cross-cell Integrated
Orchestration of Multi-slice admission. It is designed for the Open Radio Access Net-
work (O-RAN), a softwarised RAN architecture with open, standardised interfaces
between its components. One encoder, shared by every gNB, feeds a separate Q-head
for each slice, under centralised training and decentralised execution. The encoder is
a graph attention network (GAT) [38], and the per-slice heads, each a small multi-
layer perceptron (MLP), carry AXIOM’s advantage as the cluster grows. Attention
itself adds nothing measurable, as Section 6.3 shows. Three gaps found in our prior
systematic review of 67 core studies motivate pairing AXIOM with a stricter evalua-
tion, as Section 2 details. No reviewed study checks whether its outcome metric can
score a degenerate policy, such as one that accepts every request, as well as a correct

    We evaluate AXIOM in two offline simulators of a gNB cluster. Firstly, we consider
a simple environment (Env A) with a static load, where shedding every mMTC request
is close to optimal. Secondly, we consider Env B, which adds time-varying load and
inter-cell coupling, so shedding one fixed slice is no longer optimal. We then compare
AXIOM with a single-agent deep Q-network (DQN-SA), independent per-gNB deep
Q-networks (DQN-I), multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), and tuned
threshold rules. We also grow the cluster to 7, 19, and 37 gNBs under three layouts.
Further campaigns test federated training, differential privacy, and network disruption.
The contributions of this paper are as follows.
1. AXIOM, a slice-admission controller with a shared cluster encoder and decen-
   tralised per-slice Q-heads, whose parameter count does not grow with the cluster
   and whose per-slice heads, rather than attention, carry its advantage at scale.
2. Env A, a shared-budget environment with static load, built after a trivial-policy
   check showed that accepting every request beat every learned policy in our original
   simulator. In Env A, AXIOM keeps priority-slice compliance above 93% at 7, 19,
   and 37 gNBs, while both DQN baselines and MAPPO stay below 84%.
3. Env B, a more demanding environment with time-varying load and inter-cell cou-
   pling. There AXIOM leads DQN-SA, DQN-I, and MAPPO at three and nineteen
   gNBs and beats a static shedding rule at three, while a tuned threshold rule sets a
   bar that no learned policy reaches under its tuning conditions.
4. An evaluation protocol for learned slice admission control that combines trivial-
   policy checks, per-slice correctness metrics, independent-seed replication, tuned rule
   baselines, and a pre-registered analysis.
   The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related
work, Section 3 formulates the admission problem, and Section 4 presents the AXIOM
architecture. Section 5 describes the experimental setup and evaluation safeguards,
and Section 6 reports the results. Finally, Section 7 discusses the limitations, and
Section 8 concludes the paper.



2 Related Work
Our prior systematic review [18] screened 6,286 records down to 67 core studies
from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slic-
ing. The review organised them around three enabling pillars, namely topology-aware
graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federa-
tion. Each pillar is well developed on its own, but they are rarely combined under
realistic constraints, and never jointly validated in a live deployment.

    Studies from the review’s core sample show the state of each pillar. Learned
admission control uses sequential twin actor-critics [24] and digital-twin-accelerated
deep reinforcement learning [34]. Multi-agent control spans sample-efficient multi-
agent DQNs for inter-slice orchestration [8], scalable frameworks for dynamic RAN
slicing [20], and cooperative agents for energy-efficient slicing [25]. A graph neural net-
work with meta-learning makes bandwidth allocation scalable and transferable [11],
and Ahmadi et al. [2] study topology generalisation through attention. Federated deep
reinforcement learning has been applied to splitting virtual network functions [4] and
to slice mobility [22]. Two core studies combine two of the three pillars, graph atten-
tion with multi-agent learning for task offloading [23] and a graph neural network with
multi-agent learning for resource orchestration [6]. Closest to our evaluation concern,
Tsampazi et al. [37] compare many deep reinforcement learning agents for O-RAN
in an automated pipeline, a comparison that our trivial-policy and independent-seed

pillars. Like every work above, each of these evaluates its own outcome metric once,
on one seed or run.

3 System Model and Problem Formulation
This section formulates slice admission across a gNB cluster as a decentralised decision
problem and describes the two simulators that evaluate it. We consider a cluster of
N gNBs, from three in the main campaigns to 37 in the scaling study. Each gNB
serves URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block
(PRB) budget B . At each control step every gNB holds zero or more pending requests,
each tagged with a slice, and its agent accepts or rejects each one, conditioned on
the cluster state. Each decision moves the slice’s PRB-ratio ceiling, the control that
OpenAirInterface (OAI) exposes to xApps, the external control applications of O-
RAN. The binary action matches our single-gNB work, so any coordination benefit
comes from the architecture. At three gNBs the cluster is treated as fully connected,
a modelling choice, and the scaling study adds ring and hex-grid layouts. The model
works at the PRB level and abstracts the radio channel. Channel quality changes how
many PRBs a slice needs for the same traffic, and the model folds this into the per-step
demand noise. A channel-aware extension that maps the signal-to-interference-plus-
noise ratio to PRB demand is left to future work.
    The step reward follows our earlier formulations [16, 17]. It combines a per-slice
service term net of SLA-violation penalties with a congestion charge on admitted
volume and a load-imbalance penalty. For slice k , ωk is the priority weight and Rk the
per-accept reward. Its admitted count is nk and its violation penalty λk , and 1[vk ]
flags an SLA violation in that step. With these terms, the reward of step t is then

4 The AXIOM Architecture
This section presents the AXIOM architecture, its training rule, and its federated
variant. AXIOM differs from its DQN baselines in what each policy sees and shares.
AXIOM trains one encoder over the whole cluster from every agent’s experience and
reads out one Q-head per slice. DQN-SA is one controller over a flattened joint state,
and DQN-I gives each gNB its own learner over local features only. The two baselines
are therefore ablations that remove the shared encoder.

4.1 Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads
Fig. 3a shows the AXIOM architecture and its data flow. At every step each gNB forms
a feature row xi of its PRB-used ratio, congestion level, and normalised queue length,
and the rows stack into X ∈ RN ×d . A two-layer, four-head GAT over the adjacency
graph maps X to one normalised embedding zi per gNB, and Section S4 of Online
Resource 1 gives its attention equations. Every agent’s loss updates this encoder, which
is centralised training, while each agent decides its own pending requests from its own
row zi , which is decentralised execution.
    Two design choices in AXIOM matter most at scale. One small MLP per slice
gives each slice its own parameters, so URLLC’s large priority weight cannot swamp
mMTC’s smaller learning signal in a shared head, which Section 6.3 confirms at
nineteen gNBs. Each node’s raw features are also concatenated onto its normalised
embedding before the heads, Qϕk ([zi , xi ]), so a head sees absolute queue and conges-
tion levels. From here on, zi denotes that concatenated input to the heads. This skip
connection and a learning rate of 10−3 , both chosen on Env B pilot seeds before any
reported run, are the only changes from the original AXIOM design.

4.2 Federated Training with Differential Privacy
Fig. 3b shows the federated variant, in which each gNB holds a private copy of the
network, trains only on its own requests, and every Nloc local steps sends its weights
for federated averaging (FedAvg) [21]. Each client can add differential privacy through
a differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) step [1], which clips
its minibatch gradient and adds Gaussian noise of multiplier σ . The privacy loss is
accounted for with zero-concentrated differential privacy [7]. Section S4 of Online
Resource 1 gives the clipping, the privacy accounting, and its assumptions.

5 Experimental Setup
This section describes the campaigns, the baselines, and the statistical tests, followed
by the safeguards that protect every reported result. Every campaign uses 300 training
and 50 evaluation episodes per seed, where a seed fixes one network initialisation and
one environment stream, and each seed is evaluated on a held-out stream. The three-
gNB campaigns use 30 seeds, 900 to 929, with a disjoint replication sample, 1000 to
1009. The scaling campaigns use 15 seeds per layout at seven gNBs, 30 at nineteen,
and at thirty-seven 30 on the hex layout and 15 on each other layout. MAPPO [40] is
a published multi-agent baseline with a shared actor and a centralised critic, trained
on-policy for the same 300 episodes on the same reward. Its learning rate, epochs, and
minibatch size were tuned on pilot seeds, because untuned it collapsed in four of five
pilot seeds.
    Rules need no learning, and accept-all simply admits every request. A threshold
rule sheds mMTC whenever its gNB’s congestion level reaches a threshold τ , chosen
in Env A on training streams only. The selected value is τ =0, so the rule sheds every
mMTC request, and we call it the static shed-mMTC rule, or static rule for short.
It is the best rule of its family on training streams. It is a strong reference rather
than a proven optimum, since across our Env A samples learned policies exceeded it
in 28 of 450 seed-level evaluations, by at most 0.40 reward per step. In Env B we add
two tuned rule families, each chosen on training streams over a grid of thresholds and
evaluated on the held-out streams. The local family sheds mMTC on the gNB’s own
congestion or priority-slice queue and sheds eMBB on its URLLC queue, while the
neighbour-aware family also reads the mean congestion of the gNB’s neighbours. Two
further runs repeat the DQN baselines at a learning rate of 10−4 .
    All learned policies share γ = 0.95, the training budget, and the exploration sched-
ule of Table 2, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 gives the design of the sensitivity
studies.
    All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95% bootstrap percentile intervals over 10,000
resamples. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and each family of
comparisons against AXIOM is Holm-corrected for multiple testing. The test drops
exactly tied pairs, so where two policies make identical decisions on many seeds, as
AXIOM and the static rule do in Env A, a comparison rests on the remaining seeds.
    The settings, seeds, and four hypotheses of the final campaign were fixed in a pre-
registration committed to the project’s version-control history before any of its runs,
rather than lodged with an external registry. Every later campaign was pre-registered
the same way. The Env A baselines that this campaign reuses come from an earlier
rerun whose plan was committed one minute after that rerun was launched and before
any of its results were read. Section S4 of Online Resource 1 lists the reused results.
H1 is that AXIOM beats both trivial rules in Env B, and H2 compares it with every
learned baseline and tuned rule in Env B. H3 asks whether graph attention beats a
per-node MLP encoder in Env B at seven gNBs, and H4 compares AXIOM with every
baseline at nineteen gNBs in Env A.
    A pre-registered validation screen checks every trained checkpoint on its own train-
ing stream before evaluation. Every seed is reported whether or not it passes, and
Section S4 of Online Resource 1 gives the screen’s criteria.

5.1 Evaluation Safeguards
Three safeguards protect the results, and Fig. 4 places them in the evaluation protocol
with the failure each one caught. A trivial-policy check evaluates accept-all and one-
slice shedding before any learned policy is trusted, and it exposed an earlier simulator
in which accepting every request was optimal.
    Correctness-aware metrics form the second safeguard of the evaluation. Block pre-
cision π = bmMTC /b is the share of a policy’s b blocked requests that target mMTC,
and a seed that never blocks counts as a collapse rather than a success. Per-slice SLA
compliance then shows whether shedding protects URLLC and eMBB, whereas all-
slice compliance scores a correct policy and a failed one alike, because correct shedding
gives up mMTC’s SLA by design.
    Finally, every headline comparison is recomputed on an independent, disjoint seed
sample. This caught queue backlog carried over between episodes, and every result
here comes from the corrected simulator, which empties all queues at the start of each
episode.

We checked the results in three ways, with every pass criterion fixed before the run it
judged. First, rerunning eleven reported settings in fresh processes reproduced every
stored result exactly. Sixteen further reruns, from seven to thirty-seven gNBs in both

environments, also matched exactly. The one exception is DQN-SA, which builds its
network before setting the seed, and seeding it leaves AXIOM’s leads unchanged.
    Second, four further runs retrained every policy on fresh seeds, 10 to 40 per setting,
and 81 of their 90 pre-stated checks held. The nine checks that failed concern secondary
margins rather than any headline result. On fresh seeds the DQN baselines reached up
to 83.3% compliance at seven gNBs, above the 80% bound we had set, and AXIOM’s

in Env B at nineteen gNBs on 15 seeds, as Section S7 of Online Resource 1 reports.

results sentences from the raw logs. In the current version, all 688 of its checks pass.

6.7 Federation, Privacy, Disruption, and Live Deployment
Federated training costs AXIOM little reward per step on average. Pooled over all 30
federated seeds, ten of them fresh, the cost is +0.491 per step with 95% CI [−0.08,
1.09] and p = 0.14. Even so, a cost of up to about one reward per step cannot be
excluded. Per-step DP-SGD noise lowers reward by 0.77 to 1.25 per step without a
significant drop, but its formal guarantee is weak, with ε of about 2,600 or more.
Under gNB dropout and agent churn every policy loses reward significantly, and no
gap between AXIOM and any baseline survives Holm correction at ten seeds. Demand
spikes leave AXIOM’s decisions largely intact, with block precision at 0.98, and Section
S5 of Online Resource 1 gives the protocol. A live single-gNB OAI testbed confirmed
the control path that AXIOM would use, with 0.000% loss at one and at two UEs per
slice, but it never ran short of capacity, as Section S3 of Online Resource 1 reports.

7 Discussion and Limitations
No learned baseline earns significantly more reward than AXIOM in any setting we
tested, and its margin over the cheaper MAPPO is largest under time-varying load,
coupling, and uneven per-gNB load. A rule known in advance, however, matches or
beats AXIOM in steady conditions at every size, and AXIOM beats a tuned rule only
when the load moves beyond the rule’s tuning conditions. Its practical case is for
operators who cannot hand-tune and re-tune rules as conditions change, rather than
for a fixed, well-understood cluster. Fig. 10 maps this boundary over every cluster size
and load we tested.
    Several limitations bound the findings of this paper. First, both of our simulators
are synthetic rather than measured. They use a first-order coupling model with no
radio channel, user mobility, or measured traffic traces, and the live testbed never
reached contention. External validity on hardware under load therefore remains the
main open question. Second, we did not reimplement a published slice-admission algo-
rithm, so our learned baselines are standard multi-agent methods and ablations of
AXIOM rather than prior admission controllers. Third, attention added nothing over

rule that re-tunes itself online was not tested. Finally, the pre-registrations were com-
mitted to the authors’ own version-control history rather than an external registry,
and development work that changed no reported result is disclosed in Section S4 of
Online Resource 1.


8 Conclusion
This paper presented AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller with a shared
encoder and per-slice heads, evaluated under a protocol designed to make its results
trustworthy. AXIOM’s advantage over learned controllers grows with the cluster.
Under static load it reaches 98.9% of the static shed-mMTC rule’s reward at three
gNBs and keeps priority-slice compliance between 93.7% and 97.3% from seven to
thirty-seven gNBs, while every learned baseline stays below 84%. At thirty-seven gNBs
its lead over the best learned baseline reaches 35.0 points, and 36.5 points on 20 fresh
seeds, with 7,782 parameters against up to 682,058.


                                                         20
    Under time-varying load and coupling AXIOM earns 27% to 36% more reward
than MAPPO at three and nineteen gNBs, so its extra cost pays most where the
environment is dynamic. When load rises 10% to 20% above the conditions a tuned
rule was set for, AXIOM overtakes that rule at nineteen gNBs by 6.2 to 8.7 reward per
step without retraining. Simple rules still set the bar in other settings. A static rule
known in advance does better under steady load and at nineteen gNBs under coupling,
and a tuned rule beats every learned policy at its tuning conditions, as Section 7
discusses. The main findings held on fresh seeds never used before.
    More broadly, the evaluation safeguards show that learned admission control should
be judged against trivial and tuned rules. Its metrics should be tied to the objective,
and its results should hold on independent seeds under a pre-registered analysis.
    Three directions for future work follow from these limits. First, a live multi-gNB
deployment of AXIOM would test its gains under real contention. Second, giving
the learner the queue-threshold structure that the tuned rules exploit may close the
remaining gap to them. Finally, adding noise to the per-round model updates rather
than to every step would make the formal privacy guarantee meaningful.
