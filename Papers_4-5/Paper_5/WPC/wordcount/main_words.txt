AXIOM: A Shared Controller for Scalable Multi-Agent 5G Slice Admission

Manoj Prasad Kunasegran
*Wai Leong Pang*waileong.pang@taylors.edu.my

Swee King Phang
School of Engineering, Faculty of Innovation and Technology, Taylor's University, Subang Jaya, 47500, Selangor, Malaysia

5G network slicing must admit heterogeneous requests across cooperating base stations under per-slice guarantees. A static shedding rule cannot adapt to dynamic load, and a tuned threshold rule needs operator tuning for every cluster and load. We present AXIOM, a multi-agent admission controller whose encoder, shared by every gNodeB (gNB), feeds per-slice decision heads, keeping its size fixed as clusters grow. A pre-registered protocol compares it with single-agent and independent deep Q-networks (DQN), multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), and tuned rules. To demonstrate its self-learning capability, we compare AXIOM against manually tuned rules, which trade operator effort for performance. At 7, 19, and 37 gNBs, AXIOM keeps the mean cluster-level compliance of Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) and Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) above 93% when trained only at 7 gNBs, while the DQN and MAPPO baselines stay below 84%. Per gNB, it keeps about 4% to 5% more compliance than the closest of them. Under time-varying, coupled load, AXIOM reaches 85.5% to 95.5% of a tuned rule's reward, earns 26% to 36% more than MAPPO at 3 and 19 gNBs, and earns 42% more than the static rule at 3 gNBs. When load rises 10% to 20% past the rule's tuning conditions, AXIOM overtakes it by up to 8.7 reward per step without retraining. AXIOM uses 7,782 parameters at every size, against 5,122 for MAPPO and up to 682,058 for the DQNs. Its strengths are scalability and adaptation to changing load, which come from shared parameters and per-slice heads.

Introduction
Network slicing virtualises the Radio Access Network (RAN) to serve Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and Massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC), each under its own Service Level Agreement (SLA). Our earlier studies showed that deep reinforcement learning, including a deep Q-network (DQN) [1], can protect URLLC SLAs at a single congested gNodeB (gNB) [1], balance load across a gNB cluster [1], and run live on a real gNB [1]. However, those controllers were centralised, whereas a real deployment spans many gNBs that share spectrum planning and a cluster-wide load-balance objective, which makes admission control a multi-agent problem, as the system context in Fig. 1 shows. We ask what lets per-gNB admission agents scale, namely a view of the whole cluster, parameters shared across gNBs [1], or a separate decision head for each slice.

 

An operator has two simple alternatives to a learned controller, and both have limits. A static rule, such as shedding every mMTC request, needs no tuning but cannot adapt to dynamic load, and under time-varying load it fails to protect URLLC. A threshold rule tuned for the cluster performs best at the load it was tuned for, but its thresholds must be searched again whenever the cluster, its layout, or its load changes. Consider a cluster of a few dozen gNBs that covers a stadium and an industrial estate, where URLLC carries factory automation, eMBB carries video for spectators, and mMTC carries meters and sensors. On an event day the load around the stadium rises well past the level the thresholds were tuned for, and the operator would have to re-tune them for that day and again afterwards. AXIOM targets this setting with one small model, trained once on a seven-gNB cluster, that runs unchanged on a larger cluster at a 5-second control step and comes close to the tuned rule under nominal load, and when the load moves past the tuning point it overtakes the rule without re-tuning or retraining.

This paper introduces AXIOM, an acronym for Adaptive Cross-cell Integrated Orchestration of Multi-slice admission, for the Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN), a softwarised RAN architecture with open, standardised interfaces between its components. One encoder, shared by every gNB, feeds a separate Q-head for each slice, under centralised training and decentralised execution. The encoder is a graph attention network (GAT), one kind of graph neural network [1], and each per-slice head is a small multi-layer perceptron (MLP). AXIOM's advantage at scale comes from parameter sharing and from the per-slice heads, which keep one slice's reward scale from swamping another's. Three gaps found in our prior systematic review of 67 core studies motivate pairing AXIOM with a stricter evaluation, as Section 1 details. In the reviewed studies we found no check of whether the outcome metric can tell a degenerate policy, such as one that accepts every request, from a correct one, which blocks just enough low-priority requests to protect the priority slices. We also found no replication of a headline finding on an independent seed sample, and no test of whether a live testbed reaches the contention that its offline claims assume.

We evaluate AXIOM in two offline simulators of a gNB cluster. Environment A (Env A) has a static load, where shedding every mMTC request is close to optimal. Environment B (Env B) adds time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, so shedding one fixed slice is no longer optimal. We compare AXIOM with a single-agent deep Q-network (DQN-SA), independent per-gNB deep Q-networks (DQN-I), multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), and tuned threshold rules, on clusters of 3, 7, 19, and 37 gNBs under three layouts. Further campaigns test federated averaging (FedAvg), differential privacy, and network disruption. The contributions of this paper are as follows.

Related Work
 
Our prior systematic review [1] screened 6,286 records down to 67 core studies from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slicing. It organised them around three pillars, namely topology-aware graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federation. Each pillar is well developed on its own, but they are rarely combined, and we found none jointly validated in a live deployment. Our conference paper [1] validated the single-agent case live, and this paper combines the three pillars offline on clusters of up to 37 gNBs.

In the core sample, learned admission control uses sequential twin actor-critics [1] and digital-twin-accelerated deep reinforcement learning [1]. Multi-agent control spans sample-efficient multi-agent DQNs for inter-slice orchestration [1], scalable frameworks for dynamic RAN slicing [1], and cooperative agents for energy-efficient slicing [1]. A graph neural network with meta-learning makes bandwidth allocation scalable and transferable [1], and Ahmadi et al. [1] run graph-attention slicing and admission agents, without retraining, on networks four times larger than the one they trained on. Federated deep reinforcement learning has been applied to splitting virtual network functions [1] and to slice mobility [1]. Two more core studies combine two pillars, graph attention with one agent per gNB for task offloading [1] and a graph neural network inside a multi-agent orchestration framework for network-function placement [1]. Closest to our evaluation concern, Tsampazi et al. [1] compare many deep reinforcement learning agents for O-RAN in an automated pipeline. Beyond comparisons with greedy admission [1], in these core studies we found no check that a metric separates a trained policy from a trivial one, and no finding replicated on independent seeds.

 

The works cited in the rest of this section lie outside the 67 core studies. AXIOM belongs to the broader O-RAN effort to run learned control loops over standard interfaces, which Polese et al. [1] survey and which ColO-RAN [1] and Tsampazi et al. [1] exercise on large experimental platforms. Learned slice admission predates O-RAN, and Khani et al. [1] survey it for cloud RAN. OACR M [1] uses request forecasting to maximise the infrastructure provider's long-term revenue. AXIOM instead protects priority-slice SLAs. Cooperative agents usually train by value factorisation such as QMIX [1] or with a centralised critic as in MAPPO [1], and constrained multi-agent learning has been applied to O-RAN slicing at scale [1]. Recent work in this journal applies actor-critic learning [1] and double deep Q-networks [1] to related problems, and addresses delay-aware allocation [1], federated learning [1], and multi-agent relay selection [1]. In these studies too we found no report of either check. In optical networks, Doherty et al. [1] recreated five landmark reinforcement learning studies of resource allocation and found that simple tuned heuristics outperformed the published learned allocators. For slice admission, Asdikian et al. [1] attribute a learned agent's decisions to its input features with Shapley values, a view of the policy that our checks on the metric complement.

Table 1 sets AXIOM beside the five closest works, each checked against its full text. Sulaiman et al. [1] and Ahmadi et al. [1], from the same group, are the closest prior art. Both decide each slice request with two cooperating agents, one for slicing and one for admission, and both compare with policies that admit every feasible request. Ahmadi et al. also run their agents unchanged on networks 2.5 and 4 times larger than the training network, so zero-shot size transfer has a precedent. AXIOM differs in how the task is divided and in how it is tested. Its agents are the gNBs, all sharing one encoder and per-slice heads, whereas both studies divide the task by role and use a multilayer perceptron for admission, and neither states how many seeds it trained. Zangooei et al. [1] share one policy across slices of a type in a single cell and average four seeds, and Mohajer et al. [1] run one graph-attentive agent per gNB and average five seeds, but neither compares with a rule. In none of the five did we find learned admission compared with both a trivial and a tuned admission rule, or a finding replicated on a disjoint seed sample.

Robustness studies [1] test disruptive conditions, as our disruption campaign does. Zhang et al. [1] and Yasin et al. [1] are the closest precedents for periodic synchronisation and for federated learning with differential privacy, and Gong et al. [1] combine federated training with multi-agent control. As far as these papers report, each evaluates its own outcome metric without an independent replication.

System Model and Problem Formulation
 
We consider a cluster of M gNBs, from three in the main campaigns to 37 in the scaling study. The live rig and our earlier single-gNB studies use one gNB, three is the smallest fully connected cluster, and 7, 19, and 37 are the hex-grid clusters of a centre gNB with one, two, and three rings of neighbours, so each size extends the previous hex cluster by a complete ring. Each gNB serves URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block (PRB) budget M . At each control step every gNB holds zero or more pending requests, each tagged with a slice, and its agent accepts or rejects each one, conditioned on the cluster state. Each decision moves the slice's PRB-ratio ceiling, the control that the ORANSlice fork of OpenAirInterface (OAI) [1] exposes to external controllers through its E2 agent. At three gNBs the cluster is treated as fully connected, a modelling choice, and the scaling study adds ring and hex-grid layouts. The model works at the PRB level and abstracts the radio channel, folding channel quality into the per-step demand noise.

The model leaves out several features of standard slice-admission formulations. It has no session holding times or departures, so an accepted request does not occupy resources until it ends, and it reports no blocking probability or operator revenue. Each step brings M new requests, each decided once. We made these choices because the question here is how one controller trades the slices against each other under a shared budget at every control step, and departure processes, revenue terms, and channel models would each add a source of variation that the pre-registered comparisons did not control. Whether the results carry over to long-lived sessions or measured channels is left open.

Admission across the cluster forms a decentralised partially observable Markov decision process [1]. At step M the state M comprises every gNB's queues, offered demand, and slice ceilings. Agent M measures its own feature row M of M values, namely the PRB-used ratio, congestion level, and normalised queue length of each of the three slices. The shared encoder combines it with the rows of its neighbours in M , which the gNBs exchange once per control step. For each pending request it chooses M , the shared-budget dynamics move the cluster to M , and every agent receives the common reward M of Eq. 1. Each gNB has a budget of M PRBs in both simulators. The nominal shares are 30, 60, and 10 PRBs for URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC. Each slice's mean offered demand is 105% of its share, scaled by a fixed per-gNB multiplier M between 0.9 and 1.0. This puts a gNB's mean demand at about 95% to 105% of its budget. When admitted demand exceeds M , the slices share it in proportion to demand, so every admitted mMTC request takes resources from URLLC and eMBB. Unserved demand carries forward as backlog within an episode, which starts with empty queues and every slice ceiling at its nominal share. An accept raises a slice's ceiling by 5 PRB-ratio points, up to its cap, and a reject lowers it by 5 points, down to its floor, and relieves one request's demand from the backlog. Each slice then asks for the smaller of its offered demand plus backlog and its ceiling, and the offered PRBs drift 10% per step towards their mean, with noise of M . Env B keeps all of this and adds two mechanisms. Each gNB's mean demand follows M times its nominal shares, again scaled by M , with M the step and M a phase redrawn per gNB and episode. Demand thus swings between about 60% and 124% of the budget. A gNB's usable budget also shrinks with its neighbours' previous load, M , a first-order model of inter-cell interference over the same adjacency graph the encoder reads. In Env B shedding every mMTC request leaves URLLC compliance at 61.5% at three gNBs, whereas a rule that also sheds eMBB when the URLLC queue builds keeps it at 98.7%. Per-slice compliance counts a slice as compliant only when every gNB meets it, which falls to a floor in Env B at nineteen gNBs, where it no longer separates the policies, so there we also report per-gNB compliance. The AXIOM Architecture
 
AXIOM differs from its DQN baselines in what each policy sees and shares. It trains one encoder over the whole cluster from every agent's experience, a form of parameter sharing [1], and reads out one Q-head per slice. DQN-SA is one controller over a flattened joint state, and DQN-I gives each gNB its own learner over local features only. The two baselines are in effect ablations that remove the shared encoder.

 

Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads
 
Fig. 1 shows the architecture. At every step each gNB forms a feature row M of its PRB-used ratio, congestion level, and normalised queue length for each slice, and the rows stack into M with M . A two-layer, four-head GAT over the adjacency graph maps M to one normalised embedding M per gNB, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 gives its attention equations. Every agent's loss updates this encoder, which is centralised training, while each agent decides its own pending requests from its own row M . Forming M needs the feature rows of the gNBs within two hops of gNB M , since the encoder has two layers, so execution is decentralised in its decisions but relies on features exchanged between neighbours at each control step. The per-node variant of Section 1 needs only a gNB's own row.

Two design choices matter most at scale. One small MLP per slice gives each slice its own parameters, so URLLC's large priority weight cannot swamp mMTC's smaller learning signal in a shared head, a known difficulty when reward scales differ, which Section 1 confirms at nineteen gNBs. Each node's raw features are also concatenated onto its normalised embedding before the heads, M , so a head sees absolute queue and congestion levels. From here on, M denotes that concatenated input. This skip connection and a learning rate of M , both chosen on Env B pilot seeds before any reported run, are the only changes from the original AXIOM design.

In training, every pending request at gNB M , slice M is its own temporal-difference sample, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 gives the full training loop as an algorithm. Experimental Setup
 
The protocol follows established practice for evaluating reinforcement learning [1], with many seeds, interval estimates, and paired tests, and adds trivial-policy checks specific to slice admission. Unless stated otherwise, every campaign uses 300 training and 50 evaluation episodes per seed, where a seed fixes one network initialisation and one environment stream, and each seed is evaluated on a held-out stream. Env B at three gNBs uses 60 seeds and its scaling campaigns 15 per layout. Env A uses 75 seeds at three gNBs, 45 per layout at seven gNBs, 75 per layout at nineteen, and at thirty-seven 75 on the hex layout and 45 on each other layout. These samples pool the original seeds with two pre-registered extensions that were decided after earlier seeds had been analysed, and Online Resource 1 reports the original, added, and pooled samples separately. A further 30 confirmation seeds test the three- and seven-gNB verdicts on their own and enter no pooled estimate. MAPPO [1] extends proximal policy optimisation with a shared actor and a centralised critic and trains on-policy for the same 300 episodes on the same reward. Its learning rate, epochs, and minibatch size were tuned on pilot seeds, because untuned it collapsed in four of five pilot seeds.

 

Accept-all admits every request. A threshold rule sheds mMTC whenever its gNB's congestion level reaches a threshold M , chosen in Env A on training streams only. Thresholds of 0, 0.5, and 0.8 tie exactly on the training streams, and we keep the lowest, M . The rule then sheds every mMTC request, and we call it the static shed-mMTC rule, or static rule for short. In Env A, where demand runs 5% above the nominal shares, removing mMTC frees enough PRBs to keep URLLC and eMBB compliance at 99.97% or more on average, as Section 1 shows. It is a strong reference, though not a proven optimum, since across our Env A samples learned policies exceeded it in 34 of 585 seed-level evaluations, by at most 0.40 reward per step. In Env B we add two tuned rule families, each chosen on training streams over a grid of thresholds and evaluated on the held-out streams. The local family sheds mMTC on the gNB's own congestion or priority-slice queue and sheds eMBB on its URLLC queue, while the neighbour-aware family also reads the mean congestion of the gNB's neighbours. eMBB is the second slice to shed, since under Env B's load peaks shedding mMTC alone cannot protect URLLC and eMBB has the largest PRB ceiling and the next-smallest weight.

All learned policies share M , the training budget, and the exploration schedule of Table 1, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 gives the design of the sensitivity studies. All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95% bootstrap percentile intervals over 10,000 resamples. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and each family of comparisons against AXIOM is Holm-corrected for multiple testing. The test drops exactly tied pairs, so where two policies make identical decisions on many seeds, as AXIOM and the static rule do in Env A, a comparison rests on the remaining seeds.

The settings, seeds, and four hypotheses of the final campaign were fixed in a pre-registration committed to the project's version-control history before any of its runs. Every later campaign was pre-registered the same way. The Env A baselines that this campaign reuses come from an earlier rerun whose plan was committed one minute after that rerun was launched and before any of its results were read. Section S4 of Online Resource 1 lists the reused results. H1 is that AXIOM beats both trivial rules in Env B, and H2 compares it with every learned baseline and tuned rule in Env B. H3 asks whether graph attention beats a per-node MLP encoder in Env B at seven gNBs, and H4 compares AXIOM with every baseline at nineteen gNBs in Env A.

 
Three safeguards protect the results, and Fig. 1 places them in the evaluation protocol. A trivial-policy check evaluates accept-all and one-slice shedding before any learned policy is trusted, and it exposed an earlier simulator in which accepting every request was optimal. Correctness-aware metrics form the second safeguard. Block precision, M , is the share of a policy's M blocked requests that target mMTC, and a seed that never blocks counts as a collapse, not as a success. Per-slice SLA compliance then shows whether shedding protects URLLC and eMBB, whereas all-slice compliance scores a correct policy and a failed one alike, because correct shedding gives up mMTC's SLA by design. Finally, every headline comparison is recomputed on an independent, disjoint seed sample. This caught queue backlog carried over between episodes, and every result here comes from the corrected simulator, which empties all queues at the start of each episode. A pre-registered validation screen also checks every trained checkpoint on its own training stream, every seed is reported whether or not it passes, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 gives its criteria. An AI coding assistant was used for experiment scripting and analysis, as the Statements and Declarations describe, and every reported number is recomputed from the raw logs by the independent verifier of Section 1.

Results
 
We summarise per-slice compliance as priority-slice compliance, the mean of URLLC and eMBB compliance, and call a trained policy collapsed when it accepts every request. Throughout, a reward margin is a difference in mean reward per step, a compliance margin is in percentage points, and a load level is a percentage of the nominal offered demand.

Fig. 1 shows how each learned policy trains. MAPPO rises fastest because it has no exploration schedule and samples from its own policy from the first episode. The value-based learners act at random with probability M , which starts at 1.0 and reaches its floor of 0.05 only near episode 200, so their curves climb as M decays. MAPPO then plateaus below them, because its policy settles early on shedding only part of the mMTC load, and its on-policy updates do not move it further within 300 episodes. In evaluation at three gNBs it still admits 60.7% of mMTC requests, against 7.6% for AXIOM. Over the last 20 training episodes at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs AXIOM earns 4.53 and 4.55 reward per gNB per step, against 3.30 to 3.89 for the baselines. DQN-I ends those runs 0.19 and 0.23 below its own best level, whereas AXIOM is still at its best. In Env B the curves are closer together, and at seven gNBs AXIOM and DQN-SA end level.

Cluster-Size Scaling
We grow the Env A cluster from three gNBs to seven, nineteen, and thirty-seven under fully-connected, ring, and hex-grid layouts. We test the per-layout gaps on reward and on priority-slice compliance, Holm-correcting the 18 comparisons at each size. Fig. 1 traces the result across sizes, and Section S6 of Online Resource 1 gives the per-slice detail. Cluster-level compliance counts a step only when every gNB complies, so it falls as the cluster grows even for a fixed policy, and part of each baseline's decline in Fig. 1a reflects the metric and not only a failure to learn. A pre-registered re-evaluation of all 2,130 stored runs measured compliance at every gNB, each reproducing its stored result exactly. Per gNB, AXIOM keeps 98.4% to 99.3% from seven to thirty-seven gNBs and the baselines 83.6% to 95.1%. Its lead over the closest baseline is 4.2, 3.9, and 4.99 points at seven, nineteen, and thirty-seven gNBs, at Holm-adjusted M , against 16.9, 31.8, and 36.3 points at cluster level. The cluster-level gaps thus largely reflect how often one gNB of a baseline misses its SLA. Section S6 of Online Resource 1 reports the pre-registered 5-point rule for the per-gNB lead, which is missed at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs, by 1.1 points and by 0.01 points.

 

At seven gNBs, under every layout, AXIOM keeps 13.7 to 28.5 percentage points more priority-slice compliance than each baseline and earns 3.17 to 7.45 more reward per step. All 18 comparisons hold after Holm correction, at adjusted M . At nineteen gNBs the gaps widen, and they answer hypothesis H4. AXIOM keeps a pooled priority-slice compliance of 96.9% with 95% CI [95.5%, 98.1%]. Under every layout AXIOM keeps 31.3 to 41.7 percentage points more compliance than every baseline and earns 11.3 to 18.5 more reward per step, winning 70 to 75 of 75 seeds. At thirty-seven gNBs, where each baseline does not read the layout and was run once per seed, the lead grows further, and AXIOM keeps a pooled priority-slice compliance of 94.2% with 95% CI [91.9%, 96.3%]. Under every layout AXIOM keeps 33.0 to 63.4 percentage points more compliance than every baseline and earns 23.8 to 32.8 more reward per step. Here all 18 comparisons hold after Holm correction within their pre-registered families, at adjusted M . AXIOM collapses more often than at nineteen gNBs, as Fig. 1b shows, but even its collapsed runs keep 56% to 67%, the level of the best baselines. A pre-registered replication on 20 fresh seeds confirms the result. There AXIOM keeps 95.1% priority-slice compliance against 34.1% to 58.5% for the baselines and leads each on compliance and on reward at Holm-adjusted M . It collapses in 1 of the 20 seeds, against 18 for DQN-SA. Section S8 of Online Resource 1 gives throughput and normalised backlog in every setting.

Accepting every request, the natural floor at scale, keeps 71.3%, 66.9%, and 61.9% priority-slice compliance at seven, nineteen, and thirty-seven gNBs. Every baseline falls below it on average at the two larger sizes, and AXIOM stays 26 to 32 points above it. At scale AXIOM reliably learns the correct shedding policy, the one the static rule encodes, which none of DQN-SA, DQN-I, and MAPPO does. Where that rule is known, AXIOM comes within 3.2 points of it up to nineteen gNBs and within 5.8 at thirty-seven, or 5.2 on the hex layout alone, while from seven gNBs up every other learner falls at least 19 points short. The published value-decomposition network (VDN) [1] ties AXIOM at three gNBs but trails it by 13.6 points at nineteen, as Section S6 of Online Resource 1 reports. QMIX trained on a parameter-shared per-gNB MLP matched AXIOM's compliance at nineteen gNBs on ten fresh seeds, but its training loss ended 4.2 to 6.0 times above its lowest level in every seed, which fails the stability rule fixed in advance (Section S6 of Online Resource 1).

Fig. 1 shows where the gap comes from, gNB by gNB, in the thirty-seven-gNB hex cluster. A gNB's admission gate for a slice is its PRB-ratio ceiling, averaged over every evaluation step of all 75 seeds. AXIOM keeps the URLLC and eMBB gates open at every gNB, at 1.00 and 0.99, and closes the mMTC gate to 0.12 even with its six collapsed seeds included. No baseline closes the mMTC gate below 0.58.

AXIOM's encoder and heads have the same shape at every cluster size, so a policy trained at one size can run unchanged at another. A pre-registered run evaluated the seven-gNB checkpoints, never retrained, at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs, and Table 1 gives the result on the 15 original seven-gNB seeds. In Env A on the hex layout, AXIOM transferred from seven gNBs keeps 96.2% and 95.4% priority-slice compliance at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs. Accepting every request keeps 65.9% and 61.1%, and MAPPO transferred the same way keeps 64.2% and 61.5%. All four of AXIOM's leads hold at Holm-adjusted M . On 20 fresh seeds trained for this check, AXIOM moved from seven gNBs keeps 96.2% at nineteen gNBs and 95.2% at thirty-seven. It beats accept-all and MAPPO moved the same way by 27.0 to 33.8 points, at Holm-adjusted M . In Env A the transferred policy earns exactly the size-independent static rule's reward on 9 to 11 of 15 seeds.

 

Ablations and Sensitivity
 
Five checks, detailed in Sections S5 and S7 of Online Resource 1, test where AXIOM's behaviour comes from. First, the per-slice Q-heads matter as the cluster grows. A variant with one shared head of matched size ties AXIOM at three gNBs. At nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, however, it collapses in 9 of 15 seeds and keeps only 65.9% priority-slice compliance against AXIOM's 96.8% on the same seeds, losing every seed at Holm-adjusted M . Second, the gain does not depend on graph attention. In Env A at three gNBs a per-node MLP encoder earns 0.048 less reward per step than AXIOM, with 95% CI [ M 0.09, 0.17]. The paired test finds this gap significant only in the pooled sample, at Holm-adjusted M , with a Hodges-Lehmann estimate of 0.020. On the confirmation seeds AXIOM leads the MLP encoder by 0.473, at M . In Env B at seven gNBs the MLP encoder leads AXIOM instead, by 1.12 and 0.95 reward per step on the ring and hex layouts, and wins 10 of 15 seeds in each. These leads miss significance in the pre-registered test H3, at Holm-adjusted M and M . On ten fresh ring-layout seeds, however, the MLP encoder led AXIOM by 1.38 reward per step at M . Third, a variant with one joint summed target in place of per-request samples is never significantly different. Fourth, the learning rate matters, and AXIOM stays within 0.13 of the static rule's reward under changed load and slice weights. A 24-configuration MAPPO grid and a seeded DQN-SA still lose to AXIOM in every setting tested. Finally, depth-3 decision trees reproduce 86% to 97% of AXIOM's held-out decisions in Env B and shed eMBB once the URLLC queue passes 0.21 at three gNBs and 0.28 at seven after 900 training episodes, near the tuned rule's 0.2.

Time-Varying Load with Inter-Cell Coupling
 

Table 1 and Fig. 1 report Env B at three gNBs, where shedding one fixed slice no longer suffices. There AXIOM and the tuned local rule both admit about two thirds of eMBB requests and little mMTC, whereas the static rule admits every eMBB request. AXIOM earns 2.560 more reward per step than shedding every mMTC request and 4.546 more than accept-all. Both gaps hold at Holm-adjusted M , as hypothesis H1 predicted. It also leads every learned policy, by 1.121 reward per step over DQN-I, 1.338 over DQN-SA, and 1.801 over MAPPO, and keeps more priority-slice compliance than each, at Holm-adjusted M . A rule tuned on these very conditions sets the reference. The tuned rule that also sheds eMBB whenever the URLLC queue passes 0.2 earns 0.426 more than AXIOM at M , so AXIOM reaches 95.3% of its reward. The tuned neighbour-aware rule out-earns AXIOM by 0.235 per step at M , while AXIOM keeps 1.2 points more priority-slice compliance than it, at M . Section S2 of Online Resource 1 gives both verdicts by sample.

 

 

At seven gNBs AXIOM needs a longer training budget. With 300 episodes it reaches 85.5% to 86.4% of the tuned rule's reward, because 28 of AXIOM's 45 seeds settle on the static rule's policy and never shed eMBB, which none of its 60 seeds does at three gNBs. With 900 episodes for every learner on the ring layout, AXIOM keeps 55.7% priority-slice compliance against 40.5% to 42.9% for the baselines, at Holm-adjusted M , close to the tuned rule's 60.5%.

At nineteen gNBs, where cluster-level compliance saturates, AXIOM earns 5.54 to 21.22 more reward per step than every learned baseline and keeps 5.5 to 10.9 percentage points more per-gNB priority-slice compliance, at Holm-adjusted M . AXIOM reaches 98.4% of the static rule's reward there and 95.5% of the reward of the tuned local rule carried over from seven gNBs, which earn 1.31 and 3.81 more per step. Fig. 1 maps this per-gNB compliance for the four learned policies. Each hexagon is one gNB, shaded from dark at 50% to light at 85% by the mean share of evaluation steps on which it meets the URLLC and eMBB SLAs. Every policy keeps the least at the centre gNB, whose load the simulator fixes at the nominal level while it draws every other gNB's from 90% to 100% of it. The six inner gNBs have as many neighbours but trail the edge by only 0.5 to 1.8 points. AXIOM keeps more than every learned baseline at each of the nineteen gNBs, by at least 3.8 points, and by at least 4.3 points on the 15 fresh seeds. The carried-over rule leads AXIOM by 13.0 points at the centre and 6.9 points elsewhere, and by 13.2 and 7.0 points on the fresh seeds.

 

The rule's lead holds only under its tuning conditions. When the load rises to 110% to 120% without re-tuning or retraining, AXIOM wins 21 to 23 of 25 seeds at three gNBs, at Holm-adjusted M . At nineteen gNBs it earns 6.23, 7.55, and 8.67 more reward per step than that rule, winning 14 of 15 seeds at each level at Holm-adjusted M . Fresh seeds reproduce these leads, as Section S7 of Online Resource 1 reports. The static rule, which ignores the load, also overtakes the tuned rule at nineteen gNBs past its tuning load, by 8.05 to 10.63 reward per step, and its lead of 1.8 to 2.0 over AXIOM there is within chance. Past the tuning load AXIOM also out-earns MAPPO, DQN-SA, and DQN-I at seven and nineteen gNBs, at Holm-adjusted M . At three gNBs it leads all three at 110% load, and from 115% DQN-SA and DQN-I come within 0.21 reward per step of it. Matching AXIOM under shifted load takes a fresh threshold search, since only a rule re-tuned on the shifted streams ties AXIOM up to 115% load and leads it at 120%.

Model Complexity

Section S6 of Online Resource 1 tabulates the cost of each policy. AXIOM's shared encoder keeps its parameter count fixed as the cluster grows, while the DQN baselines grow with it, and its greedy decisions on one central processing unit (CPU) thread stay well inside the 5-second control period. Against MAPPO, the cheapest learner, the extra cost buys the most where the environment is dynamic. In Env B AXIOM earns 26% more reward per step than MAPPO at three gNBs and 36% more at nineteen gNBs.

Reproducibility and Fresh-Seed Verification
 
We checked the results in three ways, with every pass criterion fixed before the run it judged, except the one reused rerun disclosed in Section 1. First, rerunning eleven reported settings in fresh processes reproduced every stored result exactly. Sixteen further reruns, from seven to thirty-seven gNBs in both environments, also matched exactly.

Second, four further runs retrained every policy on fresh seeds, 10 to 40 per setting, and 81 of their 90 pre-stated checks held, including every headline result. A fifth held all three of its checks at thirty-seven gNBs on 20 seeds, and a sixth all four in Env B at nineteen gNBs on 15 seeds. A final pre-registered run held eight of its nine checks, replicating the transfer and load-shift results, and its failed check, at three gNBs past the tuning load, is reported in Section 1. Section S2 of Online Resource 1 lists the nine checks that failed, all on secondary margins, and compares each reported difference from a learned baseline with its fresh-seed counterpart, and 35 of the 39 whose CI excludes zero hold. The seed extension that doubled the main samples reproduced every headline lead on its new seeds alone. The 30 confirmation seeds then reproduced AXIOM's lead over DQN-SA at seven gNBs on the ring layout, by 1.94 reward per step and 11.8 points of compliance at Holm-adjusted M .

Third, a script written independently of the analysis pipeline, with its own log reader and Holm correction, recomputes every results table and the key results sentences from the raw logs, and all 767 of its checks pass. Section S2 of Online Resource 1 and Online Resource 2 add interquartile means and probabilities of improvement, and all 51 orderings that this paper draws from means hold under them.

Federation, Privacy, Disruption, and Live Testbed Check
 
Sections S3 to S5 of Online Resource 1 report four further checks. Federated training, in which each gNB trains a private copy and shares its weights through FedAvg [1], costs AXIOM little reward. Pooled over all 30 federated seeds, ten of them fresh, the cost is M per step with 95% CI [ M 0.08, 1.09] and M . Adding differential privacy through differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD), with the privacy loss accounted by zero-concentrated differential privacy [1], lowers reward by 0.77 to 1.25 per step without a significant drop. Noise on every step gives M of about 2,600 or more, too weak to be useful. Under gNB dropout and agent churn every policy loses reward, and AXIOM is as robust as the baselines, with no gap surviving Holm correction at ten seeds. Demand spikes leave AXIOM's block precision at 0.98. AXIOM was evaluated offline. A live single-gNB OAI testbed built on ORANSlice [1] confirmed the control path that AXIOM would use, with 0.000% loss at one and at two UEs per slice. The rig did not reach contention, so admission under load on hardware is future work.

Discussion and Limitations
 
To demonstrate the self-learning capability of AXIOM, we compared it against manually tuned rules, which trade operator effort for performance under known conditions. Under its own tuning conditions the tuned rule leads in Env B, and in Env A the static rule, which is near-optimal under static load, is never significantly beaten. AXIOM comes close to both. It reaches 98.9% of the static rule's reward in Env A at three gNBs and stays within 5 points of its compliance at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs even when trained at seven. In Env B it reaches 95.3% and 95.5% of the tuned rule's reward at three and nineteen gNBs. No learned baseline earns significantly more reward than AXIOM in any setting we tested, although AXIOM's own per-node MLP-encoder variant led it on ten fresh seeds in Env B at seven gNBs (Section 1), and its margin over the cheaper MAPPO is largest under time-varying load, coupling, and uneven per-gNB load.

AXIOM's advantages are scalability and adaptation to load. One 7,782-parameter network trained at seven gNBs serves clusters five times larger. Among the learned controllers of Fig. 1, only AXIOM meets both service thresholds at 37 gNBs and keeps above 90% compliance when moved there from seven gNBs without retraining, as Fig. 1 shows. The rules trade these properties differently. The static rule needs no tuning and runs at any size, but it cannot adapt to dynamic load. Under time-varying load at three gNBs it leaves URLLC compliance at 61.5% and AXIOM earns 42% more reward. At nineteen gNBs in Env B, however, the static rule earns slightly more than AXIOM, by 1.31 reward per step at nominal load and by a margin within chance past it, and it also overtakes the tuned rule there (Section S7 of Online Resource 1). The tuned rule needs delicate tuning, since a rule re-tuned on shifted streams selected different thresholds at every shift, and without that search AXIOM overtakes it. Fig. 1 maps this boundary over every cluster size and load we tested. In Env B AXIOM pulls ahead of the tuned rule at every size from 110% of the rule's tuning load, and from 105% at nineteen gNBs. AXIOM is not retrained after deployment, so this edge comes from training, not from online adaptation. AXIOM is most useful to operators who cannot hand-tune and re-tune rules as clusters and loads change.

 

 

Three limits bound these findings. First, both simulators are synthetic, with a first-order coupling model and no radio channel, user mobility, or measured traffic traces, and AXIOM has not yet run on a live multi-gNB cluster. Validation on hardware under load is the main next step, and future work could drive demand from public cellular activity records to test AXIOM under measured load. Second, our learned baselines are standard multi-agent methods and ablations of AXIOM, since we did not reimplement a published slice-admission algorithm. Third, the reward charges each slice's SLA penalty once for the cluster, and Section S7 of Online Resource 1 shows how a per-gNB penalty changes compliance. The pre-registrations are kept in the authors' own version-control history, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 discloses the development work that preceded the reported runs.

Conclusion
 
This paper presented AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller with a shared encoder and per-slice heads, evaluated under a pre-registered protocol. AXIOM's advantage over learned controllers grows with the cluster. Under static load it reaches 98.9% of the static shed-mMTC rule's reward at three gNBs and keeps priority-slice compliance between 94.2% and 97.7% from seven to thirty-seven gNBs, while DQN-SA, DQN-I, and MAPPO stay below 84%. These are cluster-level values, and per gNB AXIOM keeps about 4% to 5% more compliance than the closest baseline. At thirty-seven gNBs it keeps 63% more cluster-level compliance than the best learned baseline, with 7,782 parameters against 5,122 for MAPPO and up to 682,058 for DQN-I.

Under time-varying load and coupling AXIOM earns 26% to 36% more reward than MAPPO at three and nineteen gNBs, so its extra cost pays most where the environment is dynamic. When load rises 10% to 20% above the conditions a tuned rule was set for, AXIOM overtakes that rule at nineteen gNBs by 6.2 to 8.7 reward per step without retraining, and under the rule's own conditions it already reaches 95% of the rule's reward. Set against manually tuned rules, AXIOM trades a small share of the reward under known conditions for scalability across cluster sizes and adaptation to load without operator tuning. The main findings held on fresh seeds never used before. More broadly, learned admission control should be judged against trivial and tuned rules, with metrics tied to the objective and results that hold on independent seeds.

Three directions for future work follow from these limits. First, extending our live single-gNB deployment [1] to multiple gNBs would test AXIOM's gains under real contention. Second, giving the learner the queue-threshold structure that the tuned rules exploit may close the remaining gap to them. Finally, adding noise to the per-round model updates rather than to every step would make the formal privacy guarantee meaningful.
