\title[AXIOM for Multi-Agent Slice Admission Control]{AXIOM: Scalable Multi-Agent Slice Admission Control under Verified Evaluation}

\author[1]{\fnm{Manoj Prasad} \sur{Kunasegran}}\nomail

\author*[1]{\fnm{Wai Leong} \sur{Pang}}\email{waileong.pang@taylors.edu.my}

\author[1]{\fnm{Swee King} \sur{Phang}}\nomail

\affil[1]{\orgdiv{School of Engineering}, \orgname{Taylor's University}, \orgaddress{\city{Subang Jaya}, \postcode{47500}, \state{Selangor}, \country{Malaysia}}}

\abstract{Fifth generation (5G) network slicing must admit heterogeneous requests across cooperating base stations under strict per-slice guarantees. This paper presents AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller with a shared cluster encoder and one decision head per slice. We evaluate it under a pre-registered protocol in two simulators, one with static load and one with time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, against single-agent and independent deep Q-networks (DQNs), multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), and tuned threshold rules. AXIOM's advantage over learned baselines grows with cluster size. At 7, 19, and 37 gNodeBs (gNBs) it keeps Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) and enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) Service Level Agreement (SLA) compliance above 93\%, while every learned baseline stays below 84\% and only a known static rule does better. Its parameter count stays at 7,782 at any size. At three gNBs it reaches 98.9\% of an oracle rule's reward under static load, and under coupling it beats the static rule by 39\% and leads every learned baseline. Federated training shows no significant reward cost, though a moderate one remains possible. A three-parameter rule tuned with domain knowledge outperforms every learned policy under coupling at its tuning conditions, but AXIOM overtakes it when load rises beyond them. A single DQN over the joint state can match AXIOM in some settings, and graph attention adds nothing over a per-node encoder. Recomputation and pre-stated checks on fresh seeds confirm the main findings. A live OpenAirInterface (OAI) testbed validates only the control path, not AXIOM itself.}

\keywords{Network Slicing, Slice Admission Control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Graph Neural Networks, Federated Learning, Open RAN}

\section{Introduction}
Network slicing virtualises the physical radio access network (RAN) to serve heterogeneous services under Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC) categories. Each slice carries distinct Service Level Agreement (SLA) targets that are in turn based on Quality of Service (QoS) thresholds. Our earlier work~\cite{mecon-paper1,icraie-paper2} validated that a Deep Q-Network (DQN) admission controller can enforce SLAs for a single gNodeB (gNB) in a live OpenAirInterface (OAI) testbed. However, a real deployment spans multiple gNBs that share backhaul, spectrum planning, and a cluster-wide load-balance objective. Thus, slice admission at one gNB is rarely isolated, and coordinated admission is in fact a multi-gNB, multi-agent challenge. This research aims to address whether a shared, graph-structured view of the cluster could enable per-gNB agents to decide better than a single agent on a flattened state, as opposed to independent agents. Fig.~\ref{fig:sysview} places the research premise in context.

This paper introduces AXIOM, an Adaptive Cross-cell Integrity-aware O-RAN Multi-agent slice-admission controller, where O-RAN denotes the Open Radio Access Network. Integrity-aware means that every result AXIOM reports passes checks of evaluation integrity, which test whether a metric can be trusted before it is used. AXIOM is a centralised-training, decentralised-execution multi-agent architecture that is built on a shared Graph Attention Network (GAT)~\cite{wu2021gnnsurvey} encoder over a multi-gNB topology, which feeds decentralised per-slice Q-heads. We deliver it together with the evaluation discipline needed to trust what it reports. Three gaps from our systematic review of 67 core studies motivate this pairing, as Section~\ref{sec:litreview} details. First, no reviewed study checks whether its own outcome metric can score a degenerate policy as well as a correct one. Second, none replicates a headline architectural finding on an independent seed sample. Third, none tests whether a live testbed can reach the contention regime its offline claims assume. We address these gaps through a per-component realisation of the architecture, namely the GAT encoder, the Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) controller, the Federated Learning (FL) coordinator, and the Differential Privacy (DP) layer, each measured and isolated rather than folded into one end-to-end number. We apply a correctness-aware metric pair and a reproduction-versus-replication discipline throughout, and we add a live single-gNB deployment that tests, rather than assumes, whether the rig reaches genuine per-slice scarcity.

We evaluate AXIOM in two offline simulators of a gNB cluster. The first, Env A, has a static load in which a static rule that sheds every mMTC request is close to optimal. The second, Env B, adds time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, so that shedding one fixed slice is no longer optimal and neighbouring gNBs affect each other. We compare AXIOM with single-agent and independent deep Q-networks, with multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), and with tuned threshold rules. We also extend it to federated training with differential privacy, stress it under gNB dropout, demand spikes, and agent churn, and grow the cluster to 7, 19, and 37 gNBs under fully-connected, ring, and hex-grid adjacency. The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item AXIOM, a multi-agent controller with a shared cluster encoder and decentralised per-slice Q-heads, whose parameter count does not grow with the cluster. In Env A it keeps priority-slice compliance above 93\% at 7, 19, and 37 gNBs, where both DQN baselines and MAPPO stay below 84\%, and in Env B it leads every learned baseline at three and nineteen gNBs.
\item A harder environment with time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, in which AXIOM beats a static shedding rule at three gNBs and a tuned threshold rule sets a bar that no learned policy reaches under the conditions it was tuned for.
\item An evaluation protocol for learned slice admission control that combines trivial-policy checks, per-slice correctness metrics, independent-seed replication, tuned rule baselines, and a pre-registered analysis. It caught seven evaluation failures in this work, and fresh seeds confirmed the main findings, with the exceptions that Section~\ref{sec:results-verify} reports.
\item Clear bounds on the architecture's claims, namely that graph attention adds nothing over a per-node encoder and that per-step differential privacy gives only a weak guarantee.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:litreview}
Our prior systematic review~\cite{ref:access} screened 6{,}286 records down to 67 core studies from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slicing. The review organised them around three enabling pillars, namely topology-aware graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federation. Each pillar is well developed on its own, but the three are rarely combined under realistic constraints, and never with a validated live deployment. No reviewed study checks whether its own evaluation metric can mask a degenerate policy, and none replicates a headline finding on an independent seed sample, which is the gap this paper fills.

Studies from the review's core sample show the state of each pillar. For admission control, Ojijo et al.~\cite{ojijo2024sac} admit slice requests with a sequential twin actor-critic over a multi-dimensional state space and a distributed action space, and Tao et al.~\cite{tao2025dtac} use a digital twin to accelerate online deep reinforcement learning for admission. For multi-agent control, Doanis and Spyropoulos~\cite{doanis2024madqn} make multi-agent DQNs sample-efficient for multi-domain inter-slice orchestration, Li et al.~\cite{li2026scalablemarl} propose a scalable multi-agent framework for dynamic RAN slicing, and ICE-CREAM~\cite{phyu2025icecream} coordinates fully cooperative decentralised agents for energy-efficient RAN slicing. For graph representation, Hao et al.~\cite{hao2024hybridtask} combine a graph neural network with meta-learning to make bandwidth allocation scalable and transferable. For federation, Amiri et al.~\cite{amiri2024vnf} split virtual network functions in O-RAN slicing with federated deep reinforcement learning, and Ming et al.~\cite{ming2024fedslice} apply federated deep reinforcement learning to prediction-based slice mobility. Closest to our evaluation concern, Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2025pandora} design and evaluate many deep reinforcement learning agents for O-RAN in an automated pipeline, a systematic comparison that our trivial-policy and independent-seed checks extend.

AXIOM also sits inside the broader O-RAN effort to run learned control loops as applications over standard interfaces. Polese et al.~\cite{polese2023oran} survey that architecture and its open problems, while ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran} and Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2023xapps} train and compare deep reinforcement learning controllers for slicing on large experimental platforms. Learned slice admission predates O-RAN, from market-driven admission of slice requests~\cite{bega2020market} to reinforcement learning for RAN slicing~\cite{raza2019slicing}. For cooperating agents, value factorisation such as QMIX~\cite{rashid2020qmix} and centralised-critic policy gradients such as MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} are the standard training schemes, and we compare against MAPPO directly. Constrained multi-agent learning has also been applied to Open RAN slicing at scale~\cite{zangooei2024flexible}, and Khani et al.~\cite{khani2024sacsurvey} survey deep reinforcement learning for slice admission in cloud RAN. Within this journal, recent work applies actor-critic learning to joint resource allocation in RAN slicing~\cite{kulkarni2024actor}, double deep Q-networks to next-generation networks~\cite{kamal2026ddqn}, and delay-aware slice allocation to cloud RAN~\cite{alqahtani2025dynamic}. Other work in the journal applies federated learning to 6G slice resource allocation~\cite{krishnamoorthy2026federated} and multi-agent reinforcement learning to device-to-device relay selection~\cite{sirma2025relay}. None of these evaluates against a trivial-policy check or replicates its headline result on an independent seed sample. AXIOM instead shares one encoder across agents and gives every slice its own decision head, which Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} shows matters as the cluster grows.

Several recent works come close on a single axis of this problem. Shao et al.~\cite{shao2021gatmarl} motivate our GAT encoder choice by showing that graph attention strengthens inter-agent cooperation over plain DQN and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) baselines. Ahmadi et al.~\cite{ahmadi2024generalizable} study topology generalisation through graph attention, which complements but does not answer the fixed-topology evaluation-integrity question we ask in Section~\ref{sec:results-m6}. Sulaiman et al.~\cite{sulaiman2023coordinated} are the closest prior art to our joint slicing-and-admission-control problem. None of these three checks whether its reward-optimal policy could be mistaken for a degenerate one, and none replicates a headline result on an independent seed sample. Table~\ref{tab:related} sets AXIOM beside the six closest works. Two of them study admission control, four use a graph encoder, and none describes federated training, so none combines the three as AXIOM does.

Robustness studies share our concern but stop short of it. Tashman and Cherkaoui~\cite{tashman2026adversarial} and Fatehi et al.~\cite{fatehi2026attentionmarl} study robustness to a disruptive condition, as our disruption campaign in Section~\ref{sec:results-m4} does, yet neither checks whether the reported robustness metric is trustworthy and neither replicates a finding on an independent sample. That is the gap our independent-seed replication closes. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022federatedoran} are the closest periodic-synchronisation precedent, and Yasin et al.~\cite{yasin2025dpfl} are the closest precedent for federated learning with differential privacy. Their threat model differs from ours, since they secure the controller rather than characterise a control policy's own privacy-utility trade-off, which our $\sigma$ sweep in Section~\ref{sec:results-m3} reports, and neither adds a correctness-aware check on whether federating or privatising changed decision quality. A further, closely related set~\cite{mohajer2026pronto,bouroudi2025gnnmarl,qasim2025tgnnfl,gong2026femaddpg} combines two of the three pillars, yet like every work above, each evaluates on its own reported outcome metric, computed once, on one seed or run.

Rather than adding a new point in the GAT, MARL, and FL design space, this paper fills that gap with a demonstration on AXIOM, extended to federation, privacy, and disruption. It shows that two checks each changed what we report, namely testing a metric's own trustworthiness and replicating a finding on an independent sample. Skipping either would have changed what a reader believes the resulting numbers mean.

We consider a cluster of $N$ gNBs, with $N{=}3$ in the main campaigns and up to 37 in the scaling study, each serving the same three slices, URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC, under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block (PRB) budget $B$. An admission decision is a PRB-ceiling adjustment through the \texttt{slicing\_control\_m} minimum and maximum ratio, rather than a literal per-flow accept or reject, since the real OAI xApp control interface exposes only ratio ceilings. No real multi-gNB physical topology is available to us, so at three gNBs we take the least assumption-laden choice and treat the cluster as fully connected, as a modelling choice rather than a measured fact, and the scaling study adds ring and hex-grid layouts. At each control step, every gNB holds zero or more pending admission requests, each tagged with a slice identity, and an agent decides whether to accept or reject for each request independently, conditioned on the current cluster state. The discrete action space matches our single-gNB work exactly at \texttt{action\_dim}${=}2$, so any coordination benefit found here is due to the architecture and not a richer action space.

The step reward follows our prior single-gNB formulation. It has a per-slice service term net of SLA-violation penalties, a congestion charge on admitted volume, and a load-imbalance penalty across gNBs. The step reward $r_t$ sums over slices indexed by $k$. It uses priority weight $\omega_k$, per-accept reward $R_k$, admitted count $n_k$, violation penalty $\lambda_k$, and the indicator $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ equal to one exactly when slice $k$ is in SLA violation that step,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \sum_{k}\!\big(\omega_k R_k\, n_k - \lambda_k\,\mathbf{1}[v_k]\big)
      \;-\; \kappa\, C_t \lVert \mathbf{a}_t \rVert_1
      \;-\; \beta\,(1 - F_t),
\end{equation}
where $\lVert \mathbf{a}_t \rVert_1$ is the total admitted this step, $C_t$ the cluster-mean congestion level, $\kappa$ the congestion coefficient, $F_t\!\in\![0,1]$ the min-over-max PRB-utilisation fairness ratio, and $\beta$ the load-balance weight. The violation flag $v_k$ follows from a continuous SLA margin $m_k$. The correctness-aware metrics of Section~\ref{sec:collapse} also build on this margin. We take it as the worse of the queue and loss channels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin}
m_k = \min\!\Big(1 - \tilde q_k,\; 1 - \tfrac{\ell_k}{\ell_k^{\max}}\Big),
\qquad v_k \Leftrightarrow m_k \le 0,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde q_k$ the slice backlog relative to its budget, and $\ell_k$ and $\ell_k^{\max}$ its loss proxy and loss budget. The weights are set so that, under congestion, the intended policy is differentiated shedding, which keeps accepting URLLC and eMBB and sheds mMTC.

Every result uses an offline, closed-loop simulation of the cluster, except the bounded live anchor of Section~\ref{sec:results-m8}. Each gNB has a budget of $B{=}100$ PRBs shared by its three slices. Each slice's mean offered demand is 1.05 times its nominal PRB share of 30, 60, and 10 PRBs for URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC. Aggregate demand therefore exceeds the budget by about five per cent, while URLLC and eMBB demand stays below their own caps of 33 and 66 PRBs. Whenever the slices' admitted demand exceeds $B$, they share $B$ in proportion to that demand, so every admitted mMTC request takes resources from URLLC and eMBB. Unserved demand carries forward as backlog within an episode, which drives the SLA margin of Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin}. Every episode starts with empty queues, and per-gNB load multipliers drawn from $[0.9, 1.0]$ keep the cluster heterogeneous. We also use a second, harder environment, Env B, which keeps Env A and adds two mechanisms. First, each gNB's mean demand follows $0.95\,(1+0.3\sin(2\pi(s+\psi_i)/60))$ times its nominal shares, where $s$ is the step within the episode and the phase $\psi_i$ is redrawn for every gNB at every episode, so demand swings between about 0.67 and 1.24 times the budget. Second, a gNB's usable budget shrinks with its neighbours' load on the previous step, $B_i(t)=B\,(1-0.15\,\bar u_{\mathcal{N}(i)}(t-1))$, where $\bar u_{\mathcal{N}(i)}$ is the mean PRB utilisation of $i$'s neighbours in the same adjacency graph the encoder reads. This is a first-order model of inter-cell interference, which a gNB observes only through its own queues. The reward weights are unchanged from Env A. In Env B shedding every mMTC request is no longer the best rule, since it leaves URLLC compliance at 62.5\% at three gNBs, whereas a rule that also sheds eMBB when the URLLC queue builds keeps it at 98.9\%. Both constants were calibrated on pilot seeds with rules only, before any reported seed was run. Per-slice compliance counts a slice as compliant only when every gNB meets it, so in Env B it saturates at nineteen gNBs. At that size we therefore also report per-gNB compliance, the share of steps and gNBs at which a slice meets its SLA, as Section~\ref{sec:results-envb} describes.


\section{The AXIOM Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}

AXIOM differs from its two DQN baselines in exactly what each policy sees and shares. AXIOM runs one graph-attention encoder over the whole gNB cluster, trains that encoder jointly from every agent's experience, and reads out one dedicated Q-head per slice, so every gNB decides from a shared view of the cluster that the adjacency graph shapes. The single-agent DQN keeps one controller over a flattened joint state, with no per-gNB structure and no graph. The independent DQN gives each gNB its own learner that sees only local features and never shares parameters or reads the cluster topology. The two baselines are therefore ablations of AXIOM that remove the shared encoder and the graph input together, so any gap between them and AXIOM measures what those components add jointly. A third variant that keeps everything except attention isolates the graph's own contribution in Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}.

\subsection{Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads}
\label{sec:methodology-perslice}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}a shows the architecture. At every control step, each gNB forms its own per-slice feature row $\mathbf{x}_i$. The three features are PRB-used-ratio, congestion-level, and queue-length-norm. These rows stack into a joint node-feature matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ over the $N$ gNB nodes, with $d$ features each. A two-layer, $H{=}4$-head GAT~\cite{wu2021gnnsurvey} produces one embedding per gNB. Each head $h$ decomposes additive attention rather than scoring a concatenation. It uses a learned source vector $\mathbf{a}_s^{h}$ and destination vector $\mathbf{a}_d^{h}$, applied to the shared linear projection $\mathbf{W}^{h}$. Here $\mathrm{LeakyReLU}$ is the leaky rectifier of slope $0.2$, and $\mathcal{N}_i$ is the set of neighbours of node $i$ under the adjacency mask,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat}
\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}=\mathrm{LeakyReLU}\big(\mathbf{a}_s^{h\top} \mathbf{W}^{h} \mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{a}_d^{h\top} \mathbf{W}^{h} \mathbf{x}_j\big),
\qquad
\alpha_{ij}^{h}=\frac{\exp\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}_i}\exp\varepsilon_{il}^{h}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}$ is the unnormalised attention score from node $i$ to neighbour $j$ in head $h$, and $\alpha_{ij}^{h}$ is its normalisation over $i$'s neighbours, with $l$ ranging over $\mathcal{N}_i$. Hidden layers concatenate the $H$ heads under an exponential linear unit (ELU) nonlinearity, while the final layer averages them. A per-layer normalisation operator $\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)$ then rescales each node's embedding to zero mean and unit variance, and produces the final per-gNB embedding $\mathbf{z}_i$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat-agg}
\mathbf{z}_i=\mathrm{LN}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{H}\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_i}\alpha_{ij}^{h}\,\mathbf{W}^{h} \mathbf{x}_j\Big).
\end{equation}
Centralisation comes from this encoder, whose parameters every agent's loss updates jointly, so it sees the full cluster state during training. Decentralised execution comes next, since at action-selection time each agent consumes only its own embedding row $\mathbf{z}_i$ to decide its own pending requests.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{AXIOM: centralised training, decentralised execution}
\label{alg:gat-ctde}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE encoder $f_\theta$, per-slice Q-heads $\{Q_{\phi_k}\}$, targets $f_{\theta^-},\{Q_{\phi_k^-}\}$, buffer $\mathcal{D}$, adjacency $\mathbf{A}$, discount $\gamma$, sync period $T_{\mathrm{s}}$
\FOR{each step $t$, observing joint node features $\mathbf{X}_t$}
  \STATE $\mathbf{E}_t \gets f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_t, \mathbf{A})$ \COMMENT{centralised, one row per gNB}
  \STATE for each pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$, pick $a \gets \arg\max_a Q_{\phi_k}([\mathbf{E}_t[i], \mathbf{X}_t[i]])[a]$, $\epsilon$-greedy \COMMENT{decentralised, own row only}
  \STATE step env, store $(\mathbf{X}_t, \mathbf{X}_{t+1}, \{(i,k,a)\}, r_t, \text{done})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
  \IF{$|\mathcal{D}| \geq$ warmup}
    \STATE sample minibatch $\mathcal{M}$; $\mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{M}} \gets f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{A})$, $\mathbf{E}'_{\mathcal{M}} \gets f_{\theta^-}(\mathbf{X}'_{\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{A})$
    \STATE per request, $y \gets r + \gamma(1{-}\text{done})\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}(\mathbf{E}'_{\mathcal{M}}[i])[a']$
    \STATE $\mathcal{L} \gets$ mean per-request Huber loss, never summed per step; update $\theta,\{\phi_k\}$, clip grad-norm 1.0
  \ENDIF
  \STATE every $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ episodes, $\theta^-\gets\theta$, $\phi_k^-\gets\phi_k$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The \texttt{LayerNorm} and the per-slice heads are deliberate design choices. With a single shared Q-head, all three slices update the same parameters, and URLLC's large priority weight produces gradients that swamp mMTC's much smaller signal, a standard shared-parameter class imbalance. One small multi-layer perceptron (MLP) per slice, selected by the context one-hot, gives each slice its own parameters, which Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} confirms matters at nineteen gNBs, and the \texttt{LayerNorm} stops an embedding from encoding congestion through its magnitude alone. That normalisation also hides the absolute queue and congestion levels on which a state-dependent admission rule depends, so each node's raw features are concatenated onto its normalised embedding before the Q-heads, $Q_{\phi_k}([\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{x}_i])$, and below we write $\mathbf{z}_i$ for that concatenation. This skip connection, together with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ in place of the earlier $10^{-4}$, is the only change from the original AXIOM design. Both were chosen on pilot seeds of the time-varying environment of Section~\ref{sec:system-model}, where the earlier AXIOM learned only the static shedding rule, and both were fixed before any reported seed was run. A seed here is one independent random initialisation of network weights and environment stream, and we report every seed rather than a single best run.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:gat-ctde} states the training loop, where the encoder updates from every request in a sampled minibatch regardless of gNB or slice. This is centralised training, while action selection at rollout time consults only the acting agent's own embedding row, which is decentralised execution. Every pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$ is its own temporal-difference sample, and we never sum requests across a step before the loss. For a request with immediate reward $r$, next-state embedding $\mathbf{z}_i'$, and candidate next action $a'$, the bootstrapped target $y$ at discount $\gamma$ and the minibatch loss are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td}
y = r + \gamma\,(1-\mathrm{done})\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}\!\big(\mathbf{z}_i'\big)[a'],
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{(i,k,a)\in \mathcal{M}}\ell_\delta\!\Big(Q_{\phi_k}(\mathbf{z}_i)[a]-y\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\phi_k}$ is slice $k$'s online Q-head, $Q_{\phi_k^-}$ its target copy, $\mathrm{done}$ the episode-done flag, and $\mathcal{M}$ the sampled minibatch of requests. We use the Huber loss $\ell_\delta$ in place of squared error, which keeps the deeper encoder stable under long bootstraps. An earlier variant passed each gNB's summed per-request values through a QMIX-style monotonic mixer~\cite{rashid2020qmix}. Because the number of pending requests changes from step to step, that mixed target changed scale with the request count, and the mean training loss diverged, rising from about 76 over the first fifth of training steps to about 409 over the last fifth. Treating each request as its own sample removes that dependence, although Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} shows that a plain summed target works as well, so the per-request scheme is a simplification rather than a source of AXIOM's advantage.

\subsection{Federated Training with Differential Privacy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}b shows the federated variant. In this variant, each gNB becomes a federated-learning client. It holds a private copy of the same network and trains only on its own requests. Transitions are partitioned by owning agent, so no client sees another's raw admission data. Each client's GAT encoder still consumes the full joint node-feature snapshot. Every $N_{\mathrm{loc}}$ local steps, the server averages all clients' online weights by federated averaging (FedAvg)~\cite{lim2020flmec} and broadcasts the result back. We also implement FedProx~\cite{li2020flchallenges}, which adds a proximal term that pulls local weights toward the last broadcast global model. We sweep it at $\mu \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$ on three seeds. FedProx gives no consistent improvement over FedAvg, as Section~\ref{sec:results-m3} reports, so we keep FedAvg.

Differential privacy (DP) bounds how much any one client's training data can change what the shared model reveals. We apply it as a differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) step~\cite{dpsgd}. Each client clips its minibatch gradient to a fixed norm $C_{\text{dp}}$ and, when privacy is enabled, adds Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma C_{\text{dp}}$ before its optimiser step. The clip applies to the whole minibatch gradient rather than to each example, so changing any of the client's data in one step moves the clipped gradient by at most $2C_{\text{dp}}$, and the guarantee covers the client's whole step batch. We account for it with zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP)~\cite{zcdp}, where $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ are the standard privacy-loss and failure-probability parameters. Composing the Gaussian mechanism over the $m$ noisy steps a client takes gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zcdp}
\rho=\frac{2m}{\sigma^{2}},\qquad
\varepsilon(\delta)=\rho+2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)}.
\end{equation}
We credit no amplification by subsampling, because minibatches come from a growing replay buffer that reuses records, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:zcdp} is an upper bound. The clip is applied whether or not noise is added, so a $\sigma{=}0$ run and a $\sigma{>}0$ run differ only in the injected noise. The noise protects each client's own admission decisions and rewards, but not its congestion features, which every client's encoder reads from the shared node-feature snapshot by design. Each federated round therefore runs $N_{\mathrm{loc}}$ local Huber-TD steps per client with an optional FedProx proximal term $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert \theta_i - \bar\theta \rVert^2$, clips and optionally perturbs the gradient, and closes with a FedAvg average that is broadcast back.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Every campaign uses 300 training and 50 evaluation episodes per seed, and each seed is evaluated on a held-out environment stream. The three-gNB campaigns use 30 seeds, 900 to 929, in each environment, with a disjoint replication sample of seeds 1000 to 1009. The scaling campaigns use 15 seeds per cell at seven gNBs and 30 per cell at nineteen, and the thirty-seven-gNB extension uses 30 seeds on the hex layout and 15 on each other layout. We compare AXIOM with three learned baselines and several rules. The single-agent DQN is one controller over the flattened joint state. The independent DQN gives each gNB its own DQN with no parameter sharing and no topology information. Both DQN policies are ablations, deliberately reduced variants with a component removed so that its effect can be isolated. MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} is a published multi-agent baseline with a shared actor over each gNB's own features and a centralised critic over the joint state. It trains on-policy with proximal policy optimisation, for the same 300 episodes and on the same global reward. Its learning rate, epochs, and minibatch size were tuned on pilot seeds only, because untuned it collapsed into accepting every request in four of five pilot seeds, and a wider grid in Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} confirms that AXIOM's lead does not depend on this choice.

Rules need no learning, and accept-all simply admits every request. The oracle rule of Env A sheds mMTC whenever its gNB's congestion level reaches a threshold $\tau$, chosen on training streams only, and the selected value is $\tau{=}0$, so the rule sheds every mMTC request. We call it the oracle rule because it is the best rule of its family on training streams. It is a strong reference rather than a proven optimum, since across our Env A samples learned policies exceeded it in 28 of 450 seed-level evaluations, by at most 0.40 reward per step. In Env B we add two tuned rule families, each chosen on training streams over a grid of thresholds and evaluated on the held-out streams. The local family sheds mMTC on the gNB's own congestion or priority-slice queue and sheds eMBB on its URLLC queue, while the neighbour-aware family also reads the mean congestion of the gNB's neighbours. Two further arms isolate AXIOM's components, namely the DQN baselines at the lower learning rate of $10^{-4}$, and an AXIOM variant whose graph encoder is replaced by a per-node MLP of the same width that ignores the adjacency.

All learned arms share the discount factor $\gamma=0.95$, the training budget, and the exploration schedule, and AXIOM and the DQN baselines train at learning rate $10^{-3}$. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the simulation and training parameters, and Section S10 of Online Resource 1 gives every remaining hyperparameter and the design of the two sensitivity studies, which change the load and the slice weights in Env A and the learning rate and encoder width in Env B.

\subsection{Evaluation Safeguards}
\label{sec:collapse}
Three safeguards make the results below trustworthy, and Table~\ref{tab:safeguards} lists the failure each one caught during this work. Each row names the check that caught the failure and what changed as a result.

The first is a trivial-policy check, in which we evaluate the simplest rules the environment admits, accepting every request and shedding one slice outright, before trusting any learned policy. It exposed an earlier simulator in which accepting every request was optimal, and the shared-budget simulator of Section~\ref{sec:system-model} removes that loophole.

\label{sec:correctness-metrics}
The second is a correctness-aware set of metrics, computed from data every run already logs. Mean reward per step is the training objective. Block precision is the fraction of blocked requests that correctly target mMTC, the slice the reward calibration makes reject-optimal. Writing $b_{\text{mMTC}}$ and $b$ for the mMTC-targeted and total blocks over an evaluation, it is $\pi = b_{\text{mMTC}}/b$ for $b>0$ and undefined for $b=0$, so a seed that never blocks is counted as a collapse rather than scored as a success. Per-slice SLA compliance then shows whether the shedding actually protects URLLC and eMBB. We do not use the common all-slice compliance metric, because correct shedding gives up mMTC's own SLA by design, so that metric scores a correct policy and a failed one alike. Block precision applies to Env A, where mMTC is the only slice the reward makes worth shedding, and Env B is judged by reward and per-slice compliance, since its best rules also shed eMBB.

The third is a reproduction-versus-replication discipline, in which we recompute every headline comparison on an independent, disjoint seed sample and report both. It caught queue backlog that carried over between episodes, and every result in this paper comes from the corrected simulator, which empties all queues at the start of each episode.

Table~\ref{tab:m2-results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:m2-results} report the thirty-seed campaign in Env A. AXIOM never collapses, 99.2\% of its blocks land on mMTC, and it keeps URLLC and eMBB at 99.8\% and 97.9\% SLA compliance. Its mean reward of 12.698 per step is 98.9\% of the oracle rule's 12.835, and the two tie exactly in 22 of the 30 seeds. AXIOM leads the independent DQN by $+1.110$ per step, with 95\% CI [0.60, 1.65], and MAPPO by $+2.685$, with 95\% CI [2.01, 3.35], both at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0002$. Its lead over the single-agent DQN, $+0.311$ with 95\% CI [$-$0.01, 0.64], does not survive correction on thirty seeds, at $p=0.066$. MAPPO trails both DQN baselines here, because it sheds eMBB as well as mMTC, which lets eMBB compliance fall to 57.6\%.

A disjoint replication on seeds 1000 to 1009 is stronger for AXIOM. There it leads the single-agent DQN, the independent DQN, and MAPPO by $+1.083$, $+0.799$, and $+3.806$ per step at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.023$, while sitting only 0.018 below the oracle. Pooled over all 40 seeds, AXIOM leads the three baselines by $+0.504$, $+1.032$, and $+2.965$ at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.002$, and falls 0.108 short of the oracle, a small gap that is significant at $p=0.034$. Fresh seeds in Section~\ref{sec:results-verify} do not reproduce the lead over the single-agent DQN, however.

\subsection{Cluster-Size Scaling}
\label{sec:results-m6}
We grow the cluster in Env A from three gNBs to seven and nineteen under three layouts, fully-connected, ring, and hex-grid. Every gNB keeps its own shared 100-PRB budget and its own demand, so per-gNB load stays the same as the cluster grows. Each cell runs 15 seeds at seven gNBs and 30 at nineteen. A pre-registered extension then grows the cluster to thirty-seven gNBs, the three-ring hex cluster. The independent DQN ignores the layout, so it gives identical results under all three layouts, which checks that the layout changes nothing else. The single-agent DQN also ignores the layout, yet its results vary between layouts in some seeds, because its training is not bit-reproducible, for the reason Section~\ref{sec:results-verify} gives. We test the per-layout gaps on reward and on priority-slice compliance, Holm-correcting the 18 comparisons at each size.

At nineteen gNBs the gaps widen, and they answer hypothesis H4. AXIOM keeps URLLC and eMBB at 99.4\% and 95.1\%, a pooled priority-slice compliance of 97.3\% with 95\% CI [95.2\%, 99.0\%]. At thirty-seven gNBs the lead grows further, in an extension pre-registered before any of its runs. The configuration scales the arrivals exactly as before, so each gNB still sees 1.15 pending requests per step, and no setting was tuned for this size. AXIOM runs 30 seeds on the hex layout and 15 on each of the other two. The three baselines never read the layout in Env A, so each runs once on the 30 hex seeds. AXIOM keeps URLLC and eMBB at 96.7\% and 90.7\%, a pooled priority-slice compliance of 93.7\% with 95\% CI [89.8\%, 97.2\%]. The single-agent DQN, MAPPO, and the independent DQN keep 58.7\%, 56.7\%, and 32.1\%. Under every layout AXIOM leads every baseline by 32.0 to 67.8 percentage points of compliance and by 18.3 to 29.5 reward per step, and all 18 comparisons hold after Holm correction within their pre-registered families, at adjusted $p<0.01$. AXIOM collapses in 6 of its 60 runs, more often than at nineteen gNBs, against 26 of 30 for the single-agent DQN. Even its collapsed runs keep 59\% to 65\%, the level of the best baselines.

A replication on 20 fresh seeds, also pre-registered, confirms the result. There AXIOM keeps 95.1\% priority-slice compliance against 58.5\%, 57.4\%, and 34.1\% for MAPPO, the single-agent DQN, and the independent DQN, and it beats each of them on compliance and on reward at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$. It collapses in 1 of the 20 seeds, against 18 for the single-agent DQN. Section S15 of Online Resource 1 gives the setup and every arm's results.

Accepting every request is the natural floor at scale. It keeps 70.9\%, 67.1\%, and 61.9\% priority-slice compliance at seven, nineteen, and thirty-seven gNBs, and at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs every baseline falls below it on average. Their runs that do not collapse reject priority requests as well as mMTC, and at thirty-seven gNBs those runs of the single-agent and independent DQNs keep only 35.9\% and 29.1\%, so collapsing into accept-all is not the worst outcome for them. AXIOM stays 26 to 32 points above the floor. Its advantage at scale is therefore that it reliably learns the correct shedding policy, the one the static rule encodes, which the baselines fail to learn as the cluster grows, rather than coordination beyond that rule.

Tuning the DQN baselines does not close the gap. A pre-registered grid over their learning rate and minibatch size, chosen on pilot seeds, selected new settings in three of the four cells at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs. Tuned, the independent DQN rises from 56.4\% to 59.1\% at nineteen gNBs and from 32.1\% to 45.5\% at thirty-seven, while the tuned single-agent DQN at nineteen gNBs accepts every request in all 30 seeds and so matches accept-all exactly. No tuned baseline exceeds the accept-all floor, and AXIOM beats each of them on compliance and on reward at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$, by 29.9 to 48.1 points.

\subsection{Ablations and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results-ablation}
Four checks test where AXIOM's behaviour comes from. First, the learning rate matters for AXIOM and its baselines alike. In Env B the original AXIOM, at $10^{-4}$, earns 2.37 less per step than the current design in all ten seeds, while a faster rate or a wider encoder changes nothing significant, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 gives the full study.

Second, graph attention adds nothing measurable anywhere we tested it. In Env A the per-node MLP variant ties AXIOM at three gNBs, with a difference of $+0.058$ and 95\% CI [$-$0.17, 0.26], and at nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, $+0.31$ per step with 7 of 12 seeds tied. Env B's inter-cell coupling gives neighbour information real value, yet the pre-registered test H3 at seven gNBs even leans towards the MLP. The MLP keeps 1.12 and 0.95 more reward per step on the ring and hex layouts, winning 10 of 15 seeds in each, although neither difference survives Holm correction, at $p=0.061$ and $p=0.095$. AXIOM's gains therefore come from its shared encoder, per-slice heads, and joint training rather than from attention. One explanation is that a gNB's own queues already reflect most of what its neighbours do to it, so attending to them adds little.

Third, AXIOM's behaviour holds in Env A when the environment changes. Under three changed conditions, namely load at 1.02 and 1.07 times the nominal shares and the changed slice weights, AXIOM stays within 0.13 of the oracle rule's reward with no significant difference. On fresh seeds the gap at 1.02 times the load was larger in a ten-seed sample but not in a twenty-seed one, as Section~\ref{sec:results-verify} reports. On these seeds it keeps a positive lead over both DQN baselines under every condition, but at ten seeds only two of those six leads survive correction. They are its lead over the independent DQN at 1.07 times the load, $+1.073$ at $p=0.006$, and over the single-agent DQN under the changed weights, $+1.264$ at $p=0.041$.

Fourth, the per-slice Q-heads matter as the cluster grows. A variant with one shared head of matched size ties AXIOM at three gNBs in both environments. At nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, however, it collapses in 9 of 15 seeds and keeps only 65.9\% priority-slice compliance against AXIOM's 96.8\% on the same seeds, losing every seed at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$. Training on one TD sample per request, by contrast, is not what makes AXIOM work. A variant that instead regresses the sum of a step's chosen values on one joint target, as value decomposition does, is never significantly different and trends slightly better, reaching 12.831 reward per step in Env A at three gNBs and 99.96\% priority-slice compliance at nineteen. Because this was found after the reported campaigns, we tested it once on 60 fresh seeds across four cells. It was again never significantly different from AXIOM, ahead in three cells and behind at seven gNBs in Env B, so we keep the reported design.

The comparison also holds when the baselines are made stronger. A grid of 24 MAPPO configurations, chosen on pilot seeds and allowing three times the training budget, kept the reported setting in Env B and selected a longer-trained one in Env A. There MAPPO rises from 10.013 to 11.448 reward per step at three gNBs and from 60.6\% to 69.6\% priority-slice compliance at nineteen gNBs on the ring layout. AXIOM still beats it in every cell tested, by 1.25 to 16.18 reward per step at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$, and by 2.42 in Env B at seven gNBs. At thirty-seven gNBs, in a check reported but not predicted, the selected MAPPO keeps 65.6\% priority-slice compliance, and AXIOM beats it by 29.2 points and 23.23 reward per step on 15 seeds, at unadjusted $p<0.001$. With its initialisation properly seeded, the single-agent DQN keeps 80.3\% and 63.9\% priority-slice compliance at seven and nineteen gNBs, and AXIOM beats it under every layout at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.035$.

AXIOM keeps URLLC at 95.8\% by shedding eMBB as well as mMTC when URLLC queues build.

Second, among learned policies AXIOM leads at three gNBs. It beats the independent DQN, the single-agent DQN, and MAPPO by $+1.038$, $+1.337$, and $+1.863$ reward per step and also on priority-slice compliance, winning 24 to 29 of 30 seeds, all at Holm-adjusted $p<0.0001$. Third, a tuned rule beats every learned policy. The best local rule sheds every mMTC request and sheds eMBB whenever the URLLC queue passes 0.2, and it earns 9.252 per step, 0.420 more than AXIOM, winning 22 of 30 seeds at $p<0.0001$. The neighbour-aware rule earns slightly less, at 9.041, so neighbour information did not help the rules either. The rule encodes domain knowledge of which slice to shed under which pressure, which AXIOM must learn from the reward alone in 300 episodes. No policy we tested keeps eMBB compliance above 37\% in Env B, because demand at the load peaks exceeds the budget by up to 23.5\% and a slice counts as compliant only when every gNB meets it. On 15 seeds, even the rule with the highest eMBB compliance among all 95 rules of the local family, chosen in hindsight on each evaluation stream, reaches only 37.7\% on average. The best rule by reward in hindsight earns only 0.14\% more than the tuned rule.

At seven gNBs every learned policy falls further behind. The tuned rules earn 26.5 to 27.3 per step against 20.3 to 24.6 for the learned policies, and they beat AXIOM in all 15 seeds under every layout. AXIOM itself earns 23.4 to 23.6, no more than the static rule's 23.6, and keeps priority-slice compliance at 34\% to 36\%, close to the static rule. On the ring layout that is below the single-agent DQN, at Holm-adjusted $p=0.011$. AXIOM's only significant leads over a learned baseline at this size are over MAPPO's reward, under the fully-connected and ring layouts. In Env B, learned multi-agent admission control therefore beats a static rule only at three gNBs, and never beats a rule tuned with domain knowledge under the conditions that rule was tuned for.

A pre-registered run at nineteen gNBs on the hex layout, with 15 seeds, extends this comparison. Cluster-level compliance saturates there, at 16.7\% to 24.7\% for every learned policy, so we also measure per-gNB compliance, the share of steps and gNBs at which a slice meets its SLA under the framework's own per-gNB test. AXIOM leads every learned baseline on reward by 5.54 to 21.22 per step and on per-gNB priority-slice compliance by 5.5 to 10.9 points, at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.013$, keeping 77.3\%. It does not beat the static rule, which earns 1.31 more per step, and in 9 of the 15 seeds it makes exactly the static rule's decisions. The tuned local rule, carried over from seven gNBs without re-tuning, earns 3.81 more than AXIOM and keeps 84.5\% per-gNB compliance, winning every seed. Section S16 of Online Resource 1 lists every arm.

That lead depends on the conditions matching those the rule was tuned for. When the load rises after tuning and training, with neither the rule re-tuned nor AXIOM retrained, AXIOM overtakes the rule. At 1.10, 1.15, and 1.20 times the nominal load it leads the nominally tuned rule by 0.46, 0.43, and 0.35 reward per step on 25 checkpoints not used to find this effect, winning 21 to 23 of 25 seeds at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0002$. At seven gNBs on the ring layout the reversal is larger, from a deficit of 3.84 under nominal load to leads of 1.86 and 3.14 at 1.10 and 1.20 times the load. A rule re-tuned on the shifted conditions draws level with AXIOM at 1.10 and 1.15 times the load and beats it by 0.62 at 1.20. Under lighter load or stronger coupling the tuned rule keeps a lead that is not significant.

Longer training also narrows the gap under nominal conditions. With three times the training budget AXIOM gains 0.33 reward per step at three gNBs and 2.92 at seven, on 20 and 12 seeds. The remaining gaps to the tuned rule, 0.08 and 0.96 per step, are no longer significant, at $p=0.35$ and $p=0.064$.

\subsection{Federated Training and DP}
\label{sec:results-m3}

With federation switched on and no noise, mean reward per step on the ten shared seeds is 12.356 for centralised AXIOM and 11.929 for the federated variant. That is a difference of $+0.427$ with 95\% CI [$-$0.54, 1.51] and $p=0.65$, so federation shows no significant cost on these seeds, although it is more variable, with five seeds losing and four gaining. The disjoint replication shows a larger cost of $+0.968$, with 95\% CI [0.18, 1.96], which falls just short of significance at $p=0.055$. Pooled over all 30 federated seeds, including ten fresh ones, the cost is $+0.491$ per step with 95\% CI [$-$0.08, 1.09] and $p=0.14$, so federation costs little on average, but a cost of up to about one reward per step cannot be excluded.

Adding DP-SGD noise to every step lowers reward by 0.77 to 1.25 per step, and no drop survives Holm correction at ten seeds. The formal guarantee is weak at these settings, with $\varepsilon$ of about 2{,}600 or more, so per-step DP-SGD delivers no meaningful guarantee here, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 reports the privacy analysis in full.

\subsection{Disruption}
\label{sec:results-m4}
We inject three mid-episode disruptions, gNB dropout, demand spikes, and agent churn, into every policy's frozen Env A checkpoint without retraining. Dropout and churn hurt every policy significantly at every severity, and no gap between AXIOM and any baseline survives Holm correction under any disruption. Demand spikes leave AXIOM's decisions largely intact, with block precision at 0.98, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 gives the protocol and every result. With ten seeds per arm these tests detect only large gaps, so smaller differences between policies remain possible.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Fresh-Seed Verification}
\label{sec:results-verify}
We checked the results in three ways, with every pass criterion fixed before the run it judged. First, rerunning eleven reported cells on their original seeds in fresh processes reproduced every stored per-seed result exactly, with one exception. The single-agent DQN's training code builds its network before setting the seed, so the first seed of each batch starts from an unseeded initialisation. Rerunning this baseline with its initialisation seeded leaves AXIOM's leads unchanged, in Env B at three gNBs and under every Env A layout at seven and nineteen gNBs.

Second, four further runs retrained every arm on fresh seeds, 10 to 40 per cell, and 81 of their 90 pre-stated checks held. At seven and nineteen gNBs in Env A AXIOM kept 93.4\% to 100\% priority-slice compliance and beat every baseline under every layout, at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.032$ in all but one ten-seed cell, which a twenty-seed rerun then confirmed at $p\leq0.007$. In Env B at three gNBs it again beat the static rule and every learned baseline at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0002$, the tuned rules again beat every learned policy, and the disruption, federation, and privacy results held as reported. Online Resource 1 reports every number from these runs.

The nine checks that failed concern secondary margins rather than any headline result. On fresh seeds the DQN baselines reached up to 83.3\% compliance at seven gNBs, above the 80\% bound we had set. AXIOM's small lead over the single-agent DQN in Env A at three gNBs did not recur, at $-0.021$ and $+0.159$ per step on 20 and 40 new seeds, and at 1.02 times the load that baseline matched AXIOM. Two weak AXIOM seeds also left one ten-seed load sample 3.2\% short of the oracle, a gap that a twenty-seed rerun did not reproduce. A fifth fresh-seed run, at thirty-seven gNBs on 20 seeds, held all three of its checks, as Section~\ref{sec:results-m6} reports.

Third, a script written independently of the analysis pipeline, with its own log reader and Holm correction, recomputes from the raw logs the numbers in every results table and the key results sentences, and all 488 of its checks pass.

\subsection{Live Deployment}
\label{sec:results-m8}
We also checked the deployment path that AXIOM would use on a real single-gNB OAI testbed, running an offline-trained single-agent DQN checkpoint through the real E2 control loop with real UEs and per-slice User Datagram Protocol (UDP) traffic. The pipeline worked end to end, with 0.000\% loss at both loads and zero below-floor grants across more than 100{,}000 readings after a scheduler-configuration fix. Because the rig never ran short of capacity at either load, this check validates the control path but cannot show admission behaviour under contention. AXIOM itself is evaluated offline, since our two-gNB live attempt did not sustain traffic, and Section S9 of Online Resource 1 gives the testbed and every live result.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller with a shared cluster encoder and decentralised per-slice action heads, together with an evaluation protocol designed to make its results trustworthy. It targets three gaps from our prior systematic review. It addresses the first, trustworthy evaluation, tests the second, privacy-preserving coordination, and finds it still open, and validates only the control path of the third, live deployment.

AXIOM's clearest advantage appears as the cluster grows. In a static-load shared-budget environment it keeps priority-slice compliance above 93\% from seven to thirty-seven gNBs, while single-agent and independent DQNs and MAPPO stay below 84\% and only a static rule known in advance does better. Its parameter count stays at 7,782 at any size, and it reaches 98.9\% of an oracle rule's reward at three gNBs. In a harder environment with time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, AXIOM beats the static and accept-all rules and leads every learned baseline at three gNBs. At nineteen gNBs it still leads every learned baseline, although the static rule edges it there. Federated training showed no significant cost, although a moderate one cannot be excluded. These gains have limits that we state plainly. At scale, AXIOM's advantage is that it reliably learns the correct shedding policy, which the baselines fail to learn, rather than coordination beyond a known rule. A three-parameter rule tuned with domain knowledge still beats every learned policy under coupling at the conditions it was tuned for, although AXIOM overtakes it when the load rises beyond them and nearly closes the gap with three times the training. The single-agent DQN keeps pace with AXIOM in Env A at three gNBs and in Env B at seven. Graph attention added nothing over a per-node encoder, while ablations show that the per-slice heads carry AXIOM's advantage at scale. Per-step DP-SGD gave only a weak formal privacy guarantee. The main findings held on fresh seeds, and the live testbed validates the control path rather than AXIOM itself, which is evaluated offline.

The evaluation safeguards changed what this paper finally reports, exposing an inert earlier simulator, queue backlog carried over between episodes, and an AXIOM design that learned only a static rule, and every reported result comes from the corrected versions. The broader lesson is that learned admission control should be judged against trivial and tuned rules, with metrics tied to the objective, on independent seeds, under a pre-registered analysis.

Future work targets the gaps that this paper leaves open. The first is a live multi-gNB deployment of AXIOM itself. The second is closing the remaining gap to tuned rules in coupled environments, which three times the training already narrows, for instance by giving the learner the queue-threshold structure the rules exploit. The third is a privacy layer that adds noise to the per-round model updates rather than to every step, so that the formal guarantee becomes meaningful. We also plan Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) scaling, which adds compute-placement nodes to the cluster graph, cross-operator federation, and direct Quality of Experience (QoE) inference in the reward.
