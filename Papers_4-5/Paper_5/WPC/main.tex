\title[AXIOM for Federated Slice Admission Control]{AXIOM: A Correctness-Aware Graph-Attention Architecture for Federated Multi-Agent Slice Admission Control}

\author[1]{\fnm{Manoj Prasad} \sur{Kunasegran}}\nomail

\author*[1]{\fnm{Wai Leong} \sur{Pang}}\email{waileong.pang@taylors.edu.my}

\author[1]{\fnm{Swee King} \sur{Phang}}\nomail

\affil[1]{\orgdiv{School of Engineering}, \orgname{Taylor's University}, \orgaddress{\city{Subang Jaya}, \country{Malaysia}}}

\abstract{Fifth generation (5G) network slicing must admit heterogeneous service requests across many cooperating base stations under strict per-slice guarantees. This paper presents AXIOM, a correctness-aware graph-attention architecture for federated multi-agent slice admission control that closes three gaps from our prior systematic review, namely trustworthy evaluation, privacy-preserving coordination, and validated deployment. A shared Graph Attention Network (GAT) encoder reads the base-station cluster graph and feeds decentralised per-slice action heads, so each gNodeB (gNB) trains on a cluster-wide view yet decides locally. AXIOM is the only policy among three that is strong on reward, blocking accuracy, and scalability at once. It earns 11.6\% higher mean reward per step than an independent-learner baseline and blocks the correct slice 5.7\% more often. Scaled from 3 to 19 gNBs, a flattened single-agent baseline collapses into always-accept in every one of 15 test cells, whereas AXIOM cuts that collapse rate to a stable 35.4\% that is already present at 7 gNBs. Federated training adds no measurable reward cost, and differential privacy keeps blocking accuracy perfect up to a clear noise threshold. A correctness-aware metric pair and a reproduction-versus-replication discipline keep every result trustworthy, catching four evaluation-pipeline faults a standard pipeline would have reported as clean. A live deployment on OpenAirInterface (OAI) hardware serves every slice at 0.000\% loss and executes the offline-trained logic correctly end to end, establishing deployability on a real Open Radio Access Network stack. Contention-dependent behaviour is characterised in a disclosed offline stress regime calibrated harder than the live rig reaches.}

\keywords{Network Slicing, Slice Admission Control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Graph Attention Networks, Federated Learning, Open RAN}

\section{Introduction}
Network slicing virtualises physical radio access network (RAN) to serve heterogenous service delivery under Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC) categories. Each slice carries distinct Service Level Agreement (SLA) targets that are in turn based on Quality of Service (QoS) thresholds. Our earlier work~\cite{mecon-paper1,icraie-paper2} validated that a Deep Q-Network (DQN) admission controller can enforce SLAs for a single gNodeB (gNB) in a live OpenAirInterface (OAI) testbed. However, a real deployment spans multiple gNBs that share backhaul, spectrum planning, and a cluster-wide load-balance objective. Thus, slice admission at one gNB is rarely isolated, and coordinated admission is in fact a multi-gNB, multi-agent challenge. This research aims to address whether a shared, graph-structured view of the cluster could enable per-gNB agents to decide better than a single agent on a flattened state, as opposed to independent agents. Fig.~\ref{fig:sysview} places the research premise in context.

This paper introduces AXIOM, an Attention-based eXecution with Integrity for O-RAN Multi-agent slice-admission controller. AXIOM is a centralised-training, decentralised-execution multi-agent architecture that is built on a shared Graph Attention Network (GAT)~\cite{gat} encoder over a multi-gNB topology, which feeds decentralised per-slice Q-heads. We deliver it together with the evaluation discipline needed to trust what it reports. Three gaps from our systematic review of 67 core studies motivate this pairing, as Section~\ref{sec:litreview} details. First, no reviewed study checks whether its own outcome metric can score a degenerate policy as well as a correct one. Second, none replicates a headline architectural finding on an independent seed sample. Third, none tests whether a live testbed can reach the contention regime its offline claims assume. We close all three gaps through a per-component realisation of the architecture, namely the GAT encoder, the Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) controller, the Federated Learning (FL) coordinator, and the Differential Privacy (DP) layer, each measured and isolated rather than folded into one end-to-end number. We apply a correctness-aware metric pair and a reproduction-versus-replication discipline throughout, and we add a live single-gNB deployment that tests, rather than assumes, whether the rig reaches genuine per-slice scarcity.

We extend the architecture to federated training with differential privacy, and we stress it under mid-episode gNB dropout, demand spikes, agent churn, and cluster growth to $N \in \{7, 19\}$ gNBs under ring and hex-grid adjacency. On the objective it trains on, AXIOM decisively beats an independent-learner ablation and resists a training collapse that becomes total for a flattened single-agent baseline. A correctness-aware metric pair and an independent-seed replication keep every reported result trustworthy.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:litreview}
Our prior systematic review~\cite{ref:access} screened 6{,}286 records down to 67 core studies from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slicing. The review organised them around three enabling pillars, namely topology-aware graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federation. Each pillar is well developed on its own, but the three are rarely combined under realistic constraints, and never with a validated live deployment. No reviewed study checks whether its own evaluation metric can mask a degenerate policy, and none replicates a headline finding on an independent seed sample, which is the gap this paper fills.

Several recent works come close on a single axis of this problem. Shao et al.~\cite{shao2021gatmarl} motivate our GAT encoder choice by showing that graph attention strengthens inter-agent cooperation over plain DQN and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) baselines. Ahmadi et al.~\cite{ahmadi2024generalizable} study topology generalisation through graph attention, which complements but does not answer the fixed-topology evaluation-integrity question we ask in Section~\ref{sec:results-m6}. Sulaiman et al.~\cite{sulaiman2023coordinated} are the closest prior art to our joint slicing-and-admission-control problem. None of these three checks whether its reward-optimal policy could be mistaken for a degenerate one, and none replicates a headline result on an independent seed sample.

Robustness studies share our concern but stop short of it. Tashman and Cherkaoui~\cite{tashman2026adversarial} and Fatehi et al.~\cite{fatehi2026attentionmarl} study robustness to a disruptive condition, as our disruption campaign in Section~\ref{sec:results-m4} does, yet neither checks whether the reported robustness metric is trustworthy and neither replicates a finding on an independent sample. That is the gap our independent-seed replication closes, and the need is concrete, since replication overturned our initial churn-immunity reading. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022federatedoran} are the closest periodic-synchronisation precedent, and Yasin et al.~\cite{yasin2025dpfl} are the closest precedent for federated learning with differential privacy. Their threat model differs from ours, since they secure the controller rather than characterise a control policy's own privacy-utility trade-off, which our $\sigma$ sweep in Section~\ref{sec:results-m3} reports, and neither adds a correctness-aware check on whether federating or privatising changed decision quality. A further, closely related set~\cite{mohajer2026pronto,bouroudi2025gnnmarl,qasim2025tgnnfl,gong2026femaddpg} combines two of the three pillars, yet like every work above, each evaluates on its own reported outcome metric, computed once, on one seed or run.

Rather than adding a new point in the GAT, MARL, and FL design space, this paper fills that gap with a demonstration on AXIOM, extended to federation, privacy, and disruption. It shows that two checks are both necessary, namely testing a metric's own trustworthiness and replicating a finding on an independent sample. Skipping either changes what a reader may believe the resulting numbers mean.

We consider a cluster of $N{=}3$ gNBs, each serving the same three slices, URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC, under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block (PRB) budget $B$. An admission decision is a PRB-ceiling adjustment through the \texttt{slicing\_control\_m} minimum and maximum ratio, rather than a literal per-flow accept or reject, since the real OAI xApp control interface exposes only ratio ceilings. No real multi-gNB physical topology is available to us, so we take the least assumption-laden choice and treat the cluster as fully connected, as a modelling choice rather than a measured fact. At each control step, every gNB holds zero or more pending admission requests, each tagged with a slice identity, and an agent decides whether to accept or reject for each request independently, conditioned on the current cluster state. The discrete action space matches our single-gNB work exactly at \texttt{action\_dim}${=}2$, so any coordination benefit found here is due to the architecture and not a richer action space.

The step reward follows our prior single-gNB formulation. It has a per-slice service term net of SLA-violation penalties, a congestion charge on admitted volume, and a load-imbalance penalty across gNBs. The step reward $r_t$ sums over slices indexed by $k$. It uses priority weight $\omega_k$, per-accept reward $R_k$, admitted count $n_k$, violation penalty $\lambda_k$, and the indicator $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ equal to one exactly when slice $k$ is in SLA violation that step,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \sum_{k}\!\big(\omega_k R_k\, n_k - \lambda_k\,\mathbf{1}[v_k]\big)
      \;-\; \kappa\, C_t \lVert a_t \rVert_1
      \;-\; \beta\,(1 - F_t),
\end{equation}
where $\lVert a_t \rVert_1$ is the total admitted this step, $C_t$ the cluster-mean congestion level, $\kappa$ the congestion coefficient, $F_t\!\in\![0,1]$ the min-over-max PRB-utilisation fairness ratio, and $\beta$ the load-balance weight. The violation flag $v_k$ follows from a continuous SLA margin $m_k$. The correctness-aware metrics of Section~\ref{sec:collapse} also build on this margin. We take it as the worse of the queue and loss channels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin}
m_k = \min\!\Big(1 - \tilde q_k,\; 1 - \tfrac{\ell_k}{\ell_k^{\max}}\Big),
\qquad v_k \Leftrightarrow m_k \le 0,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde q_k$ the slice backlog relative to its budget, and $\ell_k$ and $\ell_k^{\max}$ its loss proxy and loss budget. These weights are tuned in prior work so that, under congestion, the reward-optimal policy is differentiated shedding, which continues accepting URLLC, mostly accepts eMBB, and rejects mMTC. We confirm this by Q-value inspection on a working baseline policy in Section~\ref{sec:methodology-perslice}, rather than assume it. Every result uses an offline, closed-loop simulation, except the bounded live anchor of Section~\ref{sec:results-m8}. The simulation is deliberately oversubscribed to roughly 110\% of one gNB's PRB budget and reuses our existing multi-gNB configuration unmodified, as a deliberately harder, disclosed stress environment. The concrete rig behind every live measurement is the single-host, software-radio, Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) stack of Fig.~\ref{fig:testbed}, which runs without a near-real-time RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC). We omit the RIC because the ORANSlice fork applies slicing control directly through a lightweight UDP E2 agent, which exercises the real per-slice PRB-ratio primitive without the overhead of a separate RIC on a single commodity host.

\section{The AXIOM Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}

AXIOM differs from its two baselines in exactly what each policy sees and shares. AXIOM runs one graph-attention encoder over the whole gNB cluster, trains that encoder jointly from every agent's experience, and reads out one dedicated Q-head per slice, so every gNB decides from a shared, topology-aware view of the cluster. The single-agent DQN keeps one controller over a flattened joint state, with no per-gNB structure and no graph. The independent DQN gives each gNB its own learner that sees only local features and never shares parameters or reads the cluster topology. The two baselines are therefore ablations of AXIOM that remove the shared encoder and the graph input in turn, so any gap between them and AXIOM measures what those two components add.

\subsection{Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads}
\label{sec:methodology-perslice}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}a shows the architecture. At every control step, each gNB forms its own per-slice feature row $x_i$. The features are PRB-used-ratio, congestion-level, and queue-length-norm. These rows stack into a joint node-feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ over the $N$ gNB nodes, with $d$ features each. A two-layer, $H{=}4$-head GAT~\cite{gat} produces one embedding per gNB. Each head $h$ decomposes additive attention rather than scoring a concatenation. It uses a learned source vector $a_s^{h}$ and destination vector $a_d^{h}$, applied to the shared linear projection $W^{h}$. Here $\phi$ is the leaky rectifier of slope $0.2$, and $\mathcal{N}_i$ is the set of neighbours of node $i$ under the adjacency mask,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat}
\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}=\phi\!\big(a_s^{h\top} W^{h} x_i + a_d^{h\top} W^{h} x_j\big),
\qquad
\alpha_{ij}^{h}=\frac{\exp\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}_i}\exp\varepsilon_{il}^{h}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}$ is the unnormalised attention score from node $i$ to neighbour $j$ in head $h$, and $\alpha_{ij}^{h}$ is its normalisation over $i$'s neighbours, with $l$ ranging over $\mathcal{N}_i$. Hidden layers concatenate the $H$ heads under an ELU nonlinearity, while the final layer averages them. A per-layer normalisation operator $\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)$ then rescales each node's embedding to zero mean and unit variance, and produces the final per-gNB embedding $z_i$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat-agg}
z_i=\mathrm{LN}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{H}\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_i}\alpha_{ij}^{h}\,W^{h} x_j\Big).
\end{equation}
Centralisation comes from this encoder, whose parameters every agent's loss updates jointly, so it sees the full cluster state during training. Decentralised execution comes next, since at action-selection time each agent consumes only its own embedding row $z_i$ to decide its own pending requests.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{AXIOM: centralised training, decentralised execution}
\label{alg:gat-ctde}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE encoder $f_\theta$, per-slice Q-heads $\{Q_{\phi_k}\}$, targets $f_{\theta^-},\{Q_{\phi_k^-}\}$, buffer $\mathcal{D}$, adjacency $A$, discount $\gamma$, sync period $C$
\FOR{each step $t$, observing joint node features $X_t$}
  \STATE $E_t \gets f_\theta(X_t, A)$ \COMMENT{centralised, one row per gNB}
  \STATE for each pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$, pick $a \gets \arg\max_a Q_{\phi_k}(E_t[i])[a]$, $\epsilon$-greedy \COMMENT{decentralised, own row only}
  \STATE step env, store $(X_t, X_{t+1}, \{(i,k,a)\}, r_t, \text{done})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
  \IF{$|\mathcal{D}| \geq$ warmup}
    \STATE sample minibatch $B$; $E_B \gets f_\theta(X_B,A)$, $E'_B \gets f_{\theta^-}(X'_B,A)$
    \STATE per request, $y \gets r + \gamma(1{-}\text{done})\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}(E'_B[i])[a']$
    \STATE $\mathcal{L} \gets$ mean per-request Huber loss, never summed per step; update $\theta,\{\phi_k\}$, clip grad-norm 1.0
  \ENDIF
  \STATE every $C$ episodes, $\theta^-\gets\theta$, $\phi_k^-\gets\phi_k$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The \texttt{LayerNorm} and the per-slice heads both answer a real training collapse, rather than a theoretical preference. An early design fed each embedding, concatenated with a one-hot slice context, into a single shared Q-head multi-layer perceptron (MLP). All three slices then update the same shared parameters. URLLC's large priority weight produces large gradients that overwrite mMTC's much smaller true signal. This is a standard shared-parameter class imbalance, and it drove the evaluation policy to block zero requests. We replace the single shared head with one small MLP per slice, selected by the context one-hot. Each slice then owns its parameters, so one slice's gradients can no longer overwrite another's. Together with the \texttt{LayerNorm}, this reduced the collapse from 30 of 30 to 9 of 30 seeds. Correctly-targeted differentiated shedding rose from 0 to 21 of the 30 seeds. A seed here is one independent random initialisation of network weights and environment stream. We report every seed rather than a single best run.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:gat-ctde} states the training loop, where the encoder updates from every request in a sampled minibatch regardless of gNB or slice. This is centralised training, while action selection at rollout time consults only the acting agent's own embedding row, which is decentralised execution. Every pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$ is its own temporal-difference sample, and we never sum requests across a step before the loss. For a request with immediate reward $r$, next-state embedding $z_i'$, and candidate next action $a'$, the bootstrapped target $y$ at discount $\gamma$ and the minibatch loss are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td}
y = r + \gamma\,(1-d)\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}\!\big(z_i'\big)[a'],
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{(i,k,a)\in B}\ell_\delta\!\Big(Q_{\phi_k}(z_i)[a]-y\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\phi_k}$ is slice $k$'s online Q-head, $Q_{\phi_k^-}$ its target copy, $d$ the episode-done flag, and $B$ the sampled minibatch of requests. We use the Huber loss $\ell_\delta$ in place of squared error, which keeps the deeper encoder stable under long bootstraps.

\subsection{Federated Training with Differential Privacy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}b shows the federated variant. Each gNB becomes a federated-learning client. It holds a private copy of the same network and trains only on its own requests. Transitions are partitioned by owning agent, so no client sees another's raw admission data. Each client's GAT encoder still consumes the full joint node-feature snapshot. Every $R$ local steps, the server averages all clients' online weights by FedAvg~\cite{fedavg} and broadcasts the result back. We also implement FedProx~\cite{fedprox}, which adds a proximal term that pulls local weights toward the last broadcast global model. We sweep it at $\mu \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$ under both homogeneous and heterogeneous per-gNB load. FedProx earns no measurable dividend at any strength, since per-round client drift is negligible relative to the loss scale.

Differential privacy gives a formal guarantee that one client's training data cannot be inferred from the shared model. We apply it as a standard differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) step~\cite{dpsgd}. Each client clips its gradient to a fixed norm $C_{\text{dp}}$. When privacy is enabled, the client then perturbs the gradient with Gaussian noise scaled by a noise multiplier $\sigma$, before its optimiser step. We report $\sigma$ as the primary privacy parameter. Alongside it we report a formal $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ bound via zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP)~\cite{zcdp}. Here $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ are the standard privacy-loss and failure-probability parameters. Composing the Gaussian mechanism over $m$ per-client gradient releases gives the zCDP parameter $\rho$ and the bound
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zcdp}
\rho=\frac{m}{2\sigma^{2}},\qquad
\varepsilon(\delta)=\rho+2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)}.
\end{equation}
We apply the same clip whether or not noise is added, so a $\sigma{=}0$ run and a $\sigma{>}0$ run differ only in whether noise is injected, which is what lets the privacy-cost comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results-m3} isolate the effect of the noise. Each federated round therefore runs $R$ local Huber-TD steps per client with an optional FedProx proximal term $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert \theta_i - \bar\theta \rVert^2$, clips and optionally perturbs the gradient, and closes with a FedAvg average that is broadcast back.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
Both campaigns use 300 training and 50 evaluation episodes per seed. The centralised campaign uses a fixed 30-seed list, seeds 900 to 929, across three arms. The first arm is AXIOM. The second is a single-agent DQN baseline run against the flattened joint state. The third is an independent-DQN ablation. An ablation is a deliberately reduced variant with one component removed, so its effect can be isolated. Here it is one per-gNB DQN with no parameter sharing and no topology information, which isolates the contribution of the shared encoder and centralised training. All three arms use matched hyperparameters, with discount factor $\gamma=0.95$ and per-episode epsilon decay. The federated campaign uses the first 10 of these seeds and a five-level noise sweep $\sigma \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4\}$. Its $\sigma{=}0$ level is therefore directly comparable to the same 10 centralised seeds. All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95\% bootstrap percentile intervals over 10{,}000 resamples, and paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The remaining settings are fixed across every seed. AXIOM trains at learning rate $1{\times}10^{-4}$ and the DQN baselines at $1{\times}10^{-3}$, both under the Adam optimiser, with epsilon decaying from $1.0$ to $0.05$ at a per-episode factor of $0.985$ and the target network synchronised every 10 episodes. The GAT encoder uses hidden and output dimension 16, feeding per-slice Q-heads of hidden dimension 32. Each federated round runs 50 local steps under FedAvg with FedProx weight $\mu{=}0$ and a differential-privacy gradient-clip norm of $1.0$.

\subsection{Evaluation Safeguards}
\label{sec:collapse}
Two safeguards make the results below trustworthy. Table~\ref{tab:safeguards} summarises the four pipeline issues they caught. A standard pipeline would have surfaced each one as a clean result. Block precision is the fraction of blocked requests that correctly target mMTC, the only slice the reward calibration ever makes reject-optimal. \section{Results}

We compare three policies throughout this section. AXIOM is the architecture of this paper, a shared graph-attention encoder that reads the whole base-station cluster and feeds one small decision head per slice. The single-agent DQN flattens the cluster into one controller that sees no per-gNB structure. The independent DQN gives each gNB its own learner that never looks at its neighbours. The two DQN policies are deliberate ablations, so any gap between them and AXIOM isolates what the shared encoder and joint training actually add.

Three quantities carry the comparison. Mean reward per step tracks the objective the policies train on, so higher is better. Block precision is the share of a policy's rejections that land on the slice the reward rule marks lowest priority, so a value near one means the policy sheds the right traffic. Collapse names a failure mode in which a trained policy stops discriminating and simply accepts every request, which can look acceptable on raw reward yet abandons admission control altogether. Table~\ref{tab:compare} sets out how the three policies stand on these axes, and the subsections that follow give the evidence.

\subsection{Centralised}
\label{sec:results-m2}

The three-gNB fully-connected setting is deliberately the hardest case for a graph encoder, since every gNB already sees every other and there is little topology left to exploit. It is therefore a clean place to establish the evaluation-integrity findings with no topology effect in play. The encoder's coordination advantage is tested in earnest only once the cluster grows and its adjacency thins, in Section~\ref{sec:results-m6}.

Table~\ref{tab:m2-results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:m2-results} report the final thirty-seed campaign. AXIOM earns the highest mean reward per step of the three policies. \subsection{Disruption}
\label{sec:results-m4}
We test every policy's frozen checkpoint against three mid-episode disruptions, injected at evaluation time with no retraining. A 'gNB dropout' forces one gNB to reject everything and blanks its row in the shared feature matrix for a window. A 'demand spike' briefly multiplies the arrival rate from outside the policy. An 'agent churn' swaps one gNB's decisions for a fresh, untrained copy of its own policy for a window and leaves the others intact. Churn applies to every genuinely multi-agent policy but not to the single-agent one. For dropout and churn the severity is how long the window lasts, at 10\%, 30\%, or 60\% of the episode. For the spike it is the arrival multiplier, at 2$\times$, 4$\times$, or 8$\times$. Because raw reward grows mechanically with request volume, we report a volume-normalised reward over an episode of $T$ steps, $\bar r = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t/\max(n_t,1)$, which divides each step reward $r_t$ by its own pending-request count $n_t$ before averaging.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_m4_disruption.pdf}
\caption{M4 disruption campaign, 10 seeds per arm. a, b, Normalised
reward cost versus severity for dropout and churn, accelerating with

Across both dropout and churn the cost accelerates with severity rather than rising in proportion, with 9 or 10 of every 10 seeds hurt and Wilcoxon $p\leq0.03$. For AXIOM the dropout cost grows 0.165, 0.601, 1.710 across the three severities, and the churn cost grows 0.059, 0.200, 0.529. Every policy shows the same accelerating shape, so this is a property of the task rather than of any one design. The demand spike leaves decision quality intact on the volume-invariant block-precision metric in Fig.~\ref{fig:m4-disruption}c, so its apparent reward gain is a counting effect, not a better policy.

One disruption result needed a second, independent sample to report honestly. An early pass read the independent DQN as immune to churn, since it never attends to another agent's features, but that rested on a small and non-significant ten-seed sample. A disjoint replication overturns it, with a churn cost that is significant at every severity and in fact larger than the coordinated policies', rising 0.097, 0.657, 1.223 against the earlier 0.000, 0.168, 0.640. The honest conclusion is that churn hurts every policy, and that the earlier immunity claim was a small-sample artifact. This was the single correction among four headline cross-section comparisons re-run against independent samples. The other three held with overlapping intervals, namely AXIOM over the independent DQN on reward at $+1.461$ then $+0.767$ with $p<0.0001$ in both, AXIOM over the single-agent policy at $+0.195$ then $+0.445$, and a negligible federation cost in both.

\subsection{Cluster-Size Scaling}
\label{sec:results-m6}
We grow the cluster from three gNBs to seven and nineteen under three layouts, fully-connected, ring, and hex-grid, scaling the synthetic arrivals with the cluster so per-gNB load stays roughly constant. Only AXIOM's decision head actually reads this layout, so the two DQN policies double as a check that nothing depends on topology by accident. We normalise reward per gNB with the same volume-normalised $\bar r$ as in Section~\ref{sec:results-m4}, and we lean on block precision rather than compliance here, because compliance is combined across gNBs with a logical OR and drifts to zero as the cluster grows whatever the policy does. The main sample is twelve seeds at nineteen gNBs across every layout and policy, with further independent seeds to test generalisation.

At nineteen gNBs the per-gNB reward margins are not significant for any layout, and that is expected rather than disappointing. The single-agent policy's always-accept collapse banks the service reward on every request, while AXIOM pays a real price each time it correctly rejects low-priority mMTC traffic. The true separation therefore shows up in collapse resistance and block precision, not in raw reward.

\subsection{Live Deployment}
\label{sec:results-m8}
This section reports what the real OAI testbed of Fig.~\ref{fig:testbed}, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:system-model}, can and cannot establish about the offline-trained policy. We run a single-agent-DQN checkpoint, seed 900, against a real gNB, real User Equipments (UEs), and real per-slice User Datagram Protocol (UDP) traffic, at one UE per slice and again at two UEs per slice.

A capacity probe at a deliberately wide-open ceiling showed all three slices serving essentially 100\% of offered demand with 0.000\% loss at both loads. The frozen checkpoint's decision logic runs correctly end to end, through the real E2 control loop, with real accept and reject decisions, and its signature low-priority-only blocking of mMTC reappears at both loads, which establishes deployability end to end. Because the rig never actually runs short of capacity, the same pattern is equally consistent with the policy's trained priority bias, so we claim admission-control behaviour under contention only in the disclosed offline stress regime, not live. Two integrity issues surfaced and were fixed during these checks. A scheduler-configuration error had turned the per-slice PRB-ratio ranges into raw grants below the gNB scheduler's hard five-PRB floor, which we fixed at the root, after which direct instrumentation confirmed zero below-floor grants across more than 100{,}000 readings. A separate unit mismatch in the live SLA-margin diagnostic was corrected by converting backlog into a queueing-delay estimate, using Little's law, against each slice's own latency budget.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented AXIOM, a correctness-aware graph-attention architecture for federated multi-agent slice admission control. A shared GAT encoder reads the cluster graph and feeds decentralised per-slice action heads. The design closes three gaps from our prior systematic review, namely trustworthy evaluation, privacy-preserving coordination, and validated deployment.

The architecture delivers on each of these fronts. AXIOM beats the independent-learner ablation decisively on the training objective, resists at 19 gNBs a collapse that overwhelms the single-agent baseline, and holds its reward under federation while differential privacy stays accurate up to a clear noise threshold. A live single-gNB deployment confirms that the offline-trained logic executes correctly on real hardware. These results carry honest bounds, since the multi-gNB, federated, and disruption campaigns are offline, the graph models only the gNB cluster, and the federated setting assumes a single operator.

Two safeguards keep these results trustworthy. A correctness-aware metric pair ties evaluation to the training objective, while a reproduction-versus-replication discipline tests each headline finding on an independent seed sample. Together they caught issues a standard pipeline would have reported as clean. The broader lesson is that evaluation here needs metrics tied to the objective, that architectural claims need an independent sample before they are trusted, and that a live testbed's reach must be checked rather than assumed.

Future work targets the remaining gaps from our review. The first goal is a full-scale simulation with real contention, so admission-control behaviour under genuine scarcity can be measured directly rather than in the disclosed stress regime. The second is Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) scaling, which adds compute-placement nodes to the cluster graph and extends the controller to joint admission and offloading. We also plan a live multi-gNB deployment, broader privacy settings across operators, and direct Quality of Experience (QoE) inference in the reward. Together these steps move the architecture toward a scalable, privacy-aware, and user-centric controller for 5G and Beyond-5G networks.
