\title[AXIOM for Scalable Multi-Agent 5G Slice Admission]{AXIOM: A Shared Graph-Attention Controller for Scalable Multi-Agent 5G Slice Admission}

\author[1]{\fnm{Manoj Prasad} \sur{Kunasegran}}\nomail

\author*[1]{\fnm{Wai Leong} \sur{Pang}}\email{waileong.pang@taylors.edu.my}

\author[1]{\fnm{Swee King} \sur{Phang}}\nomail

\affil[1]{\orgdiv{School of Engineering, Faculty of Innovation and Technology}, \orgname{Taylor's University}, \orgaddress{\city{Subang Jaya}, \postcode{47500}, \state{Selangor}, \country{Malaysia}}}

\abstract{Fifth generation (5G) network slicing must admit heterogeneous requests across cooperating base stations under strict per-slice guarantees. However, none of the 67 studies in our review tests learned controllers against trivial policies or on independent seeds. We present AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller whose graph-attention encoder, shared by every gNodeB (gNB), feeds one decision head per slice, keeping its size fixed as clusters grow. A pre-registered protocol with both checks compares it with a single-agent deep Q-network (DQN-SA), independent per-gNB deep Q-networks (DQN-I), multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO), value decomposition, and tuned rules, under static and coupled time-varying load. At 7, 19, and 37 gNBs AXIOM keeps Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) and enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) Service Level Agreement (SLA) compliance above 93\%, while every learned baseline stays below 84\% and only a known static rule does better. At 37 gNBs it leads the best learned baseline by 35.0 points, 36.5 on 20 fresh seeds. There it serves 99.8\% of URLLC demand with a backlog delay 7 to 44 times shorter than the learned baselines, using 7,782 parameters against up to 682,058. Under coupling it earns 27\% to 36\% more reward than MAPPO at 3 and 19 gNBs and 39\% more than a static shedding rule at three. When load rises 10\% to 20\% past a tuned rule's conditions, AXIOM overtakes that rule by up to 8.7 reward per step. AXIOM is therefore a viable alternative for hands-off, self-optimising slice admission in next-generation 5G and 6G networks.}

\keywords{Network Slicing, Slice Admission Control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Graph Neural Networks, Federated Learning, Open RAN}

\section{Introduction}
Network slicing virtualises the radio access network (RAN) to serve Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC), each under its own Service Level Agreement (SLA). Our earlier simulation studies showed that deep reinforcement learning, including a deep Q-network (DQN), can protect URLLC SLAs at a single congested gNodeB (gNB)~\cite{mecon-paper1} and can balance load across a gNB cluster~\cite{icraie-paper2}. Those controllers, however, were centralised, whereas a real deployment spans many gNBs that share spectrum planning and a cluster-wide load-balance objective. Consequently, admission control across such a cluster becomes a multi-agent problem. We ask whether a shared view of the cluster lets per-gNB agents decide better than one agent on a flattened state or independent agents, and Fig.~\ref{fig:sysview} places this question in context.

This paper introduces AXIOM, an Adaptive Cross-cell Integrity-aware O-RAN Multi-agent slice-admission controller, where O-RAN denotes the Open Radio Access Network and integrity-aware means that every result it reports passes checks of evaluation integrity. One encoder, shared by every gNB, feeds a separate Q-head for each slice, under centralised training and decentralised execution. The encoder is a Graph Attention Network (GAT)~\cite{wu2021gnnsurvey}, and the per-slice heads, each a small multi-layer perceptron (MLP), carry AXIOM's advantage as the cluster grows. Three gaps from our systematic review of 67 core studies motivate pairing AXIOM with an evaluation discipline, as Section~\ref{sec:litreview} details. No reviewed study checks whether its outcome metric can score a degenerate policy as well as a correct one, none replicates a headline finding on an independent seed sample, and none tests whether a live testbed reaches the contention its offline claims assume.

We evaluate AXIOM in two offline simulators of a gNB cluster. In Env A, with static load, shedding every mMTC request is close to optimal. Env B adds time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, so shedding one fixed slice is no longer optimal. We compare AXIOM with a single-agent deep Q-network (DQN-SA), independent per-gNB deep Q-networks (DQN-I), multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO), and tuned threshold rules, grow the cluster to 7, 19, and 37 gNBs under three adjacency layouts, and test federated training, differential privacy, and disruption. The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item AXIOM, a multi-agent controller with a shared cluster encoder and decentralised per-slice Q-heads, whose parameter count does not grow with the cluster and whose per-slice heads, rather than attention, carry its advantage at scale. In Env A it keeps priority-slice compliance above 93\% at 7, 19, and 37 gNBs, where both DQN baselines and MAPPO stay below 84\%, and in Env B it leads DQN-SA, DQN-I, and MAPPO at three and nineteen gNBs.
\item A harder environment with time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, in which AXIOM beats a static shedding rule at three gNBs and a tuned threshold rule sets a bar that no learned policy reaches under the conditions it was tuned for.
\item An evaluation protocol for learned slice admission control that combines trivial-policy checks, per-slice correctness metrics, independent-seed replication, tuned rule baselines, and a pre-registered analysis. It caught seven evaluation failures in this work, and fresh seeds confirmed the main findings, with the exceptions that Section~\ref{sec:results-verify} reports.
\item Clear bounds on the architecture's claims, namely that graph attention adds nothing over a per-node encoder and that per-step differential privacy gives only a weak guarantee.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:system-model} formulates the admission problem, and Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the AXIOM architecture. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup and evaluation safeguards, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:litreview}
Our prior systematic review~\cite{ref:access} screened 6{,}286 records down to 67 core studies from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slicing. The review organised them around three enabling pillars, namely topology-aware graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federation. Each pillar is well developed on its own. However, the three are rarely combined under realistic constraints, and never with a validated live deployment.

Studies from the review's core sample show the state of each pillar. Learned admission control uses sequential twin actor-critics~\cite{ojijo2024sac}, digital-twin-accelerated deep reinforcement learning~\cite{tao2025dtac}, and request forecasting to maximise the infrastructure provider's long-term revenue, as in OACR$^{2}$~\cite{li2025oacr2}. AXIOM instead protects priority-slice SLAs, the objective of an operator bound by strict per-slice guarantees. Multi-agent control spans sample-efficient multi-agent DQNs for inter-slice orchestration~\cite{doanis2024madqn}, scalable frameworks for dynamic RAN slicing~\cite{li2026scalablemarl}, and cooperative agents for energy-efficient slicing~\cite{phyu2025icecream}. A graph neural network with meta-learning makes bandwidth allocation scalable and transferable~\cite{hao2024hybridtask}, and federated deep reinforcement learning has been applied to splitting virtual network functions~\cite{amiri2024vnf} and to slice mobility~\cite{ming2024fedslice}. Closest to our evaluation concern, Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2025pandora} compare many deep reinforcement learning agents for O-RAN in an automated pipeline, a comparison that our trivial-policy and independent-seed checks extend.

AXIOM also sits inside the broader O-RAN effort to run learned control loops over standard interfaces, which Polese et al.~\cite{polese2023oran} survey and which ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran} and Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2023xapps} exercise on large experimental platforms. Learned slice admission predates O-RAN, from market-driven admission~\cite{bega2020market} to reinforcement learning for RAN slicing~\cite{raza2019slicing}, and Khani et al.~\cite{khani2024sacsurvey} survey it for cloud RAN. Cooperative agents usually train by value factorisation such as QMIX~\cite{rashid2020qmix} or with a centralised critic as in MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo}, and constrained multi-agent learning has been applied to Open RAN slicing at scale~\cite{zangooei2024flexible}. Recent work in this journal applies actor-critic learning~\cite{kulkarni2024actor}, double deep Q-networks~\cite{kamal2026ddqn}, delay-aware allocation~\cite{alqahtani2025dynamic}, federated learning~\cite{krishnamoorthy2026federated}, and multi-agent relay selection~\cite{sirma2025relay} to related problems, but none checks its metric against a trivial policy or replicates on independent seeds. AXIOM instead shares one encoder across agents and gives every slice its own decision head, which Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} shows matters as the cluster grows.

Several recent works come close on a single axis of this problem. Shao et al.~\cite{shao2021gatmarl} show that graph attention strengthens inter-agent cooperation, and Ahmadi et al.~\cite{ahmadi2024generalizable} study topology generalisation through attention. Sulaiman et al.~\cite{sulaiman2023coordinated} are the closest prior art to joint slicing and admission control, yet none of the three checks for degenerate policies or replicates on independent seeds. Table~\ref{tab:related} sets AXIOM beside the six closest works, and none of them combines admission control, a graph encoder, and federated training as AXIOM does.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:system-model}
This section formulates slice admission across a gNB cluster as a decentralised decision problem and describes the two simulators that evaluate it.

We consider a cluster of $N$ gNBs, three in the main campaigns and up to 37 in the scaling study, each serving URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block (PRB) budget $B$. At each control step every gNB holds zero or more pending requests, each tagged with a slice, and its agent accepts or rejects each one, conditioned on the cluster state. Each decision moves the slice's PRB-ratio ceiling, the control that the xApp interface of OpenAirInterface (OAI) exposes, and the binary action matches our single-gNB work, so any coordination benefit comes from the architecture. At three gNBs the cluster is treated as fully connected, a modelling choice, and the scaling study adds ring and hex-grid layouts. The model works at the PRB level and abstracts the radio channel. Channel quality changes how many PRBs a slice needs for the same traffic, and the model folds this into the per-step demand noise, so a channel-aware extension that maps the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio to PRB demand is left to future work.

The step reward follows our earlier formulations~\cite{mecon-paper1,icraie-paper2}, with a per-slice service term net of SLA-violation penalties, a congestion charge on admitted volume, and a load-imbalance penalty. With priority weight $\omega_k$, per-accept reward $R_k$, admitted count $n_k$, violation penalty $\lambda_k$, and an indicator $\mathbf{1}[v_k]$ of an SLA violation of slice $k$ that step, it is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \sum_{k}\!\big(\omega_k R_k\, n_k - \lambda_k\,\mathbf{1}[v_k]\big)
      \;-\; \kappa\, C_t \lVert \mathbf{a}_t \rVert_1
      \;-\; \beta\,(1 - F_t),
\end{equation}
where $\lVert \mathbf{a}_t \rVert_1$ is the total admitted this step, $C_t$ the cluster-mean congestion level, $\kappa$ the congestion coefficient, $F_t\!\in\![0,1]$ the min-over-max PRB-utilisation fairness ratio, and $\beta$ the load-balance weight. The violation flag $v_k$ follows from a continuous SLA margin $m_k$. The correctness-aware metrics of Section~\ref{sec:collapse} also build on this margin. We take it as the worse of the queue and loss channels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin}
m_k = \min\!\Big(1 - \tilde q_k,\; 1 - \tfrac{\ell_k}{\ell_k^{\max}}\Big),
\qquad v_k \Leftrightarrow m_k \le 0,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde q_k$ the slice backlog relative to its budget, and $\ell_k$ and $\ell_k^{\max}$ its loss proxy and loss budget. The weights are set so that, under congestion, the intended policy is differentiated shedding, which keeps accepting URLLC and eMBB and sheds mMTC.

Admission is therefore a decentralised partially observable Markov decision process. At step $t$ the state $s_t$ comprises every gNB's queues, offered demand, and slice ceilings, and agent $i$ observes its own feature row $\mathbf{x}_i$, which the shared encoder combines with the other rows of $\mathbf{X}_t$. For each pending request it chooses $a\in\{\text{accept},\text{reject}\}$, the shared-budget dynamics move the cluster to $s_{t+1}$, and every agent receives the common reward $r_t$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}. The agents jointly seek a policy $\pi$ that maximises the discounted return over an episode of $T{=}60$ steps,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t} r_t\Big],\qquad \gamma=0.95.
\end{equation}

Each gNB has $B{=}100$ PRBs, and each slice's mean offered demand is 105\% of its nominal share of 30, 60, and 10 PRBs for URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC, scaled by a fixed per-gNB multiplier between 0.9 and 1.0. A gNB's mean demand is therefore about 95\% to 105\% of its budget, before per-step noise and backlog. When admitted demand exceeds $B$, the slices share it in proportion to demand, so every admitted mMTC request takes resources from URLLC and eMBB, and unserved demand carries forward as backlog within an episode. Env B keeps all of this and adds two mechanisms. Each gNB's mean demand follows $0.95\,(1+0.3\sin(2\pi(s+\psi_i)/60))$ times its nominal shares, with $s$ the step and $\psi_i$ a phase redrawn per gNB and episode, again scaled by the per-gNB multiplier, so demand swings between about 60\% and 124\% of the budget. A gNB's usable budget also shrinks with its neighbours' previous load, $B_i(t)=B\,(1-0.15\,\bar u_{\mathcal{N}(i)}(t-1))$, a first-order model of inter-cell interference over the same adjacency graph the encoder reads. In Env B shedding every mMTC request leaves URLLC compliance at 62.5\% at three gNBs, whereas a rule that also sheds eMBB when the URLLC queue builds keeps it at 98.9\%. Per-slice compliance counts a slice as compliant only when every gNB meets it, which saturates in Env B at nineteen gNBs, so there we also report per-gNB compliance. Section S10 of Online Resource 1 gives the remaining simulator details.


\section{The AXIOM Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}
This section presents the AXIOM architecture, its training rule, and its federated variant.

AXIOM differs from its DQN baselines in what each policy sees and shares. AXIOM trains one encoder over the whole cluster from every agent's experience and reads out one Q-head per slice. DQN-SA is one controller over a flattened joint state, and DQN-I gives each gNB its own learner over local features only. The two baselines are therefore ablations that remove the shared encoder.

\subsection{Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads}
\label{sec:methodology-perslice}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}a shows the AXIOM architecture and its data flow. At every step each gNB forms a feature row $\mathbf{x}_i$ of its PRB-used ratio, congestion level, and normalised queue length, and the rows stack into $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$. A two-layer, four-head GAT over the adjacency graph maps $\mathbf{X}$ to one normalised embedding $\mathbf{z}_i$ per gNB, and Section S10 of Online Resource 1 gives its attention equations. Every agent's loss updates this encoder, which is centralised training, while each agent decides its own pending requests from its own row $\mathbf{z}_i$, which is decentralised execution.



Two design choices in AXIOM matter most at scale. One small MLP per slice gives each slice its own parameters, so URLLC's large priority weight cannot swamp mMTC's smaller learning signal in a shared head, which Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} confirms at nineteen gNBs. Each node's raw features are also concatenated onto its normalised embedding before the heads, $Q_{\phi_k}([\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{x}_i])$, so a head sees absolute queue and congestion levels, and below $\mathbf{z}_i$ denotes that concatenation. This skip connection and a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, both chosen on Env B pilot seeds before any reported run, are the only changes from the original AXIOM design.

In training, every pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$ is its own temporal-difference sample, and Section S10 of Online Resource 1 gives the full training loop. For a request with immediate reward $r$ and next-state embedding $\mathbf{z}_i'$, the target $y$ at discount $\gamma$ and the minibatch loss are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td}
y = r + \gamma\,(1-\mathrm{done})\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}\!\big(\mathbf{z}_i'\big)[a'],
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{(i,k,a)\in \mathcal{M}}\ell_\delta\!\Big(Q_{\phi_k}(\mathbf{z}_i)[a]-y\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\phi_k^-}$ is slice $k$'s target Q-head, $\mathrm{done}$ the episode-done flag, $\mathcal{M}$ the minibatch of requests, and $\ell_\delta$ the Huber loss. Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} shows that a summed per-step target works as well, so the per-request scheme is a simplification rather than a source of AXIOM's advantage.

\subsection{Federated Training with Differential Privacy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}b shows the federated variant, in which each gNB holds a private copy of the network, trains only on its own requests, and every $N_{\mathrm{loc}}$ local steps sends its weights for federated averaging (FedAvg)~\cite{lim2020flmec}. Each client can add differential privacy through a differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) step~\cite{dpsgd}, which clips its minibatch gradient and adds Gaussian noise of multiplier $\sigma$, accounted for with zero-concentrated differential privacy~\cite{zcdp}. Section S10 of Online Resource 1 gives the clipping, the privacy accounting, and its assumptions.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
This section describes the campaigns, the baselines, and the statistical tests, followed by the safeguards that protect every reported result.

Every campaign uses 300 training and 50 evaluation episodes per seed, where a seed fixes one network initialisation and one environment stream, and each seed is evaluated on a held-out stream. The three-gNB campaigns use 30 seeds, 900 to 929, with a disjoint replication sample, 1000 to 1009. The scaling campaigns use 15 seeds per layout at seven gNBs, 30 at nineteen, and at thirty-seven 30 on the hex layout and 15 on each other layout. MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} is a published multi-agent baseline with a shared actor and a centralised critic, trained on-policy for the same 300 episodes on the same reward. Its learning rate, epochs, and minibatch size were tuned on pilot seeds, because untuned it collapsed in four of five.

Rules need no learning, and accept-all simply admits every request. The oracle rule of Env A sheds mMTC whenever its gNB's congestion level reaches a threshold $\tau$, chosen on training streams only, and the selected value is $\tau{=}0$, so the rule sheds every mMTC request. We call it the oracle rule because it is the best rule of its family on training streams. It is a strong reference rather than a proven optimum, since across our Env A samples learned policies exceeded it in 28 of 450 seed-level evaluations, by at most 0.40 reward per step. In Env B we add two tuned rule families, each chosen on training streams over a grid of thresholds and evaluated on the held-out streams. The local family sheds mMTC on the gNB's own congestion or priority-slice queue and sheds eMBB on its URLLC queue, while the neighbour-aware family also reads the mean congestion of the gNB's neighbours. Two further arms rerun the DQN baselines at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$.

All learned arms share $\gamma=0.95$, the training budget, and the exploration schedule. Section S10 of Online Resource 1 lists every simulation and training parameter and the design of the sensitivity studies.



All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95\% bootstrap percentile intervals over 10{,}000 resamples. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and each family of comparisons against AXIOM is Holm-corrected for multiple testing. The test drops exactly tied pairs, so where two policies make identical decisions on many seeds, as AXIOM and the oracle rule do in Env A, a comparison rests on the remaining seeds.

The settings, seeds, and four hypotheses of the final campaign were fixed in a pre-registration committed to the project's version-control history before any of its runs, rather than lodged with an external registry, and every later campaign was pre-registered the same way. The Env A baselines that this campaign reuses come from an earlier rerun whose plan was committed one minute after that rerun was launched and before any of its results were read. Section S13 of Online Resource 1 lists the commit identifiers. H1 is that AXIOM beats both trivial rules in Env B, and H2 compares it with every learned baseline and tuned rule in Env B. H3 asks whether graph attention beats a per-node MLP encoder in Env B at seven gNBs, and H4 compares AXIOM with every baseline at nineteen gNBs in Env A.

A pre-registered validation screen checks every trained checkpoint of every learned arm on its own training stream before evaluation, and every seed is reported whether or not it passes, with the screen's criteria in Section S10 of Online Resource 1.

\subsection{Evaluation Safeguards}
\label{sec:collapse}
Three safeguards guard the results, and Table~\ref{tab:safeguards} lists the failure each one caught. A trivial-policy check evaluates accept-all and one-slice shedding before any learned policy is trusted, and it exposed an earlier simulator in which accepting every request was optimal.


\label{sec:correctness-metrics}
Correctness-aware metrics form the second safeguard of the evaluation. Block precision $\pi = b_{\text{mMTC}}/b$ is the share of a policy's $b$ blocked requests that target mMTC, and a seed that never blocks counts as a collapse rather than a success. Per-slice SLA compliance then shows whether shedding protects URLLC and eMBB, whereas all-slice compliance scores a correct policy and a failed one alike, because correct shedding gives up mMTC's SLA by design.

Finally, every headline comparison is recomputed on an independent, disjoint seed sample. This caught queue backlog carried over between episodes, and every result here comes from the corrected simulator, which empties all queues at the start of each episode.

\subsection{Static Load, Three gNBs}
\label{sec:results-m2}

Table~\ref{tab:m2-results} reports the thirty-seed campaign in Env A. AXIOM never collapses, 99.2\% of its blocks land on mMTC, and it keeps URLLC and eMBB at 99.8\% and 97.9\% SLA compliance. Its mean reward of 12.698 per step is 98.9\% of the oracle rule's 12.835, and the two tie exactly in 22 of the 30 seeds. AXIOM earns 1.110 more reward per step than DQN-I, with 95\% CI [0.60, 1.65], and 2.685 more than MAPPO, with 95\% CI [2.01, 3.35], both at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0002$. Its margin over DQN-SA, 0.311 with 95\% CI [$-$0.01, 0.64], does not survive correction on thirty seeds, at $p=0.066$. MAPPO trails both DQN baselines here, because it sheds eMBB as well as mMTC, which lets eMBB compliance fall to 57.6\%.

A disjoint replication on seeds 1000 to 1009 and the pooled 40 seeds favour AXIOM more strongly, as Section S12 of Online Resource 1 reports. Nonetheless, fresh seeds in Section~\ref{sec:results-verify} do not reproduce the lead over DQN-SA.

Accepting every request is the natural floor at scale. It keeps 70.9\%, 67.1\%, and 61.9\% priority-slice compliance at seven, nineteen, and thirty-seven gNBs, and at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs every baseline falls below it on average. Tuned, DQN-I rises from 56.4\% to 59.1\% at nineteen gNBs and from 32.1\% to 45.5\% at thirty-seven, while the tuned DQN-SA at nineteen gNBs accepts every request in all 30 seeds and so matches accept-all exactly. No tuned baseline exceeds the accept-all floor, and AXIOM beats each of them on compliance and on reward at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$, by 29.9 to 48.1 points.

Similarly, harder contention leaves this ranking of the learned policies intact. At 110\% and 120\% of the nominal load at nineteen gNBs, AXIOM keeps 63.4\% and 21.7\% priority-slice compliance, 24.3 to 34.0 and 2.5 to 6.4 percentage points more than every learned baseline, at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.006$. At 120\% the priority demand exceeds the slice caps at most gNBs, so no policy can protect it throughout.

At thirty-seven gNBs every baseline falls again and the lead reaches 35.0 points. The static rule that sheds every mMTC request keeps 99.96\% to 99.99\% at every size. \subsection{Ablations and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results-ablation}
Four checks test where AXIOM's behaviour comes from. First, the learning rate matters for AXIOM and its baselines alike. In Env B the original AXIOM, at $10^{-4}$, earns 2.37 less per step than the current design in all ten seeds, while a faster rate or a wider encoder changes nothing significant, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 gives the full study.

Second, graph attention adds nothing measurable, so a per-node MLP encoder could replace it. In Env A that variant ties AXIOM, which earns 0.058 more reward per step at three gNBs with 95\% CI [$-$0.17, 0.26] and 0.31 more at nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, with 7 of 12 seeds tied. In Env B at seven gNBs, the pre-registered test H3, it keeps 1.12 and 0.95 more reward per step on the ring and hex layouts, winning 10 of 15 seeds in each, at Holm-adjusted $p=0.061$ and $p=0.095$. On ten fresh seeds its lead was significant. AXIOM's gains therefore come from its shared encoder, per-slice heads, and joint training rather than from attention. AXIOM's results at nineteen and thirty-seven gNBs all use the attention encoder, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 lists the variant's results.

Third, AXIOM's behaviour holds in Env A when the environment changes. Under 102\% and 107\% load and changed slice weights it stays within 0.13 of the oracle rule's reward with no significant difference, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 gives its comparisons with the baselines.

Fourth, the per-slice Q-heads matter as the cluster grows. A variant with one shared head of matched size ties AXIOM at three gNBs in both environments. At nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, however, it collapses in 9 of 15 seeds and keeps only 65.9\% priority-slice compliance against AXIOM's 96.8\% on the same seeds, losing every seed at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$. Training on one temporal-difference sample per request, by contrast, is not what makes AXIOM work. A variant that instead regresses the sum of a step's chosen values on one joint target, as value decomposition does, is never significantly different and trends slightly better, reaching 12.831 reward per step in Env A at three gNBs and 99.96\% priority-slice compliance at nineteen. Because this was found after the reported campaigns, we tested it once on 60 fresh seeds across four cells. It was again never significantly different from AXIOM, ahead in three cells and behind at seven gNBs in Env B, so we keep the reported design.

The comparison also holds when the baselines are made stronger. A grid of 24 MAPPO configurations, chosen on pilot seeds and allowing three times the training budget, kept the reported setting in Env B and selected a longer-trained one in Env A. There MAPPO rises from 10.013 to 11.448 reward per step at three gNBs and from 60.6\% to 69.6\% priority-slice compliance at nineteen gNBs on the ring layout. AXIOM still beats it in every cell tested, by 1.25 to 16.18 reward per step at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$, and by 2.42 in Env B at seven gNBs. At thirty-seven gNBs, in a check reported but not predicted, the selected MAPPO keeps 65.6\% priority-slice compliance, and AXIOM keeps 29.2 percentage points more compliance and earns 23.23 more reward per step on 15 seeds, at unadjusted $p<0.001$. With its initialisation properly seeded, DQN-SA keeps 80.3\% and 63.9\% priority-slice compliance at seven and nineteen gNBs, and AXIOM beats it under every layout at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.035$.

The tuned rules earn 26.5 to 27.3 per step and beat AXIOM in all 15 seeds under every layout, while AXIOM earns 23.4 to 23.6, no more than the static rule's 23.6. Its only significant leads over a learned baseline at this size are over MAPPO's reward, and on the ring layout its compliance is below DQN-SA's, at Holm-adjusted $p=0.011$.

A pre-registered run at nineteen gNBs on the hex layout, with 15 seeds, extends this comparison. Cluster-level compliance saturates there, at 16.7\% to 24.7\% for every learned policy, so we also measure per-gNB compliance, the share of steps and gNBs at which a slice meets its SLA under the framework's own per-gNB test. AXIOM earns 5.54 to 21.22 more reward per step than every learned baseline and keeps 5.5 to 10.9 percentage points more per-gNB priority-slice compliance, at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.013$, keeping 77.3\%. It does not beat the static rule, which earns 1.31 more per step, and in 9 of the 15 seeds it makes exactly the static rule's decisions. The tuned local rule, carried over from seven gNBs without re-tuning, earns 3.81 more than AXIOM and keeps 84.5\% per-gNB compliance, winning every seed. Section S16 of Online Resource 1 lists every arm.

That lead depends on the conditions matching those the rule was tuned for. When the load rises after tuning and training, with neither the rule re-tuned nor AXIOM retrained, AXIOM overtakes the rule. At three gNBs this test used 25 checkpoints not used to find the effect. At 110\%, 115\%, and 120\% of the nominal load AXIOM earns 0.46, 0.43, and 0.35 more reward per step than the nominally tuned rule, winning 21 to 23 of 25 seeds at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0002$. At seven gNBs on the ring layout the reversal is larger. AXIOM trails the rule by 3.84 reward per step under nominal load but leads it by 1.86 and 3.14 at 110\% and 120\% load. A rule re-tuned on the shifted conditions draws level with AXIOM at 110\% and 115\% load and beats it by 0.62 reward per step at 120\%. Under lighter load or stronger coupling the tuned rule keeps a lead that is not significant.

The overload result also holds at nineteen gNBs, for the same 15 checkpoints and the rule carried over from seven gNBs. At 110\%, 115\%, and 120\% of the nominal load AXIOM earns 6.23, 7.55, and 8.67 more reward per step than that rule, winning 14 of 15 seeds at each level at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$. The rule keeps more per-gNB compliance by shedding eMBB, and the static rule earns 1.8 to 2.0 more reward than AXIOM, which makes the static rule's decisions in 9 to 11 of the 15 seeds. When per-gNB load differs widely instead, with multipliers drawn between 75\% and 125\% at seven gNBs, AXIOM does not beat the nominally tuned rule, earning 0.36 more reward per step at $p=0.23$. It ties a rule re-tuned to these conditions, while the static rule earns 0.27 more at $p=0.028$. It still beats every learned baseline there, by 1.18 to 4.28 reward per step at unadjusted $p\leq0.005$. Charging the SLA penalty per violating gNB, so that it grows with the cluster, did not raise AXIOM's per-gNB compliance, at 79.2\% against 77.3\% with $p=0.65$, and AXIOM still did not beat the static rule. Under that reward every learned baseline kept 13.7 to 18.5 percentage points less per-gNB compliance than AXIOM, in every seed. Section S17 of Online Resource 1 lists every arm of these runs.

With three times the training budget AXIOM also comes within reach of the tuned rule under nominal conditions, as Section S14 of Online Resource 1 reports.

\subsection{Throughput and Delay}
\label{sec:results-kpi}
Re-running the stored evaluations with a recorder, which reproduced every stored log that could be checked, gives each slice's throughput and a backlog-based delay. The delay is the mean backlog divided by the mean served PRBs, in control periods of 5 s, a fluid-model proxy rather than packet latency. At thirty-seven gNBs in Env A, AXIOM serves 99.8\% of the offered URLLC demand and 99.0\% of the eMBB demand, against 94.2\% to 97.7\% and 85.7\% to 97.5\% for the learned baselines. Its URLLC delay is 0.08 control periods with a 95th percentile of 0.42, whereas the baselines reach 0.58 to 3.61 with 95th percentiles of 5.2 to 16.9, and the static rule reaches 0.03. In Env B at nineteen gNBs, AXIOM's URLLC delay of 1.35 control periods is below every learned baseline's 2.17 to 2.66, while the tuned rule's is lower still, at 0.51. Section S18 of Online Resource 1 lists every cell.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Fresh-Seed Verification}
\label{sec:results-verify}
We checked the results in three ways, with every pass criterion fixed before the run it judged. First, rerunning eleven reported cells in fresh processes reproduced every stored result exactly, except DQN-SA, which builds its network before setting the seed, and seeding it leaves AXIOM's leads unchanged.

Second, four further runs retrained every arm on fresh seeds, 10 to 40 per cell, and 81 of their 90 pre-stated checks held. At seven and nineteen gNBs in Env A AXIOM kept 93.4\% to 100\% priority-slice compliance and beat every baseline under every layout, at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.032$ in all but one ten-seed cell, which a twenty-seed rerun then confirmed at $p\leq0.007$. In Env B at three gNBs it again beat the static rule and every learned baseline at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.0002$, the tuned rules again beat every learned policy, and the disruption, federation, and privacy results held as reported. Online Resource 1 reports every number from these runs.

The nine checks that failed concern secondary margins rather than any headline result. On fresh seeds the DQN baselines reached up to 83.3\% compliance at seven gNBs, above the 80\% bound we had set. AXIOM's small lead over DQN-SA in Env A at three gNBs did not recur, at margins of $-0.021$ and $0.159$ reward per step on 20 and 40 new seeds, and at 102\% load that baseline matched AXIOM. Two weak AXIOM seeds also left one ten-seed load sample 3.2\% short of the oracle, a gap that a twenty-seed rerun did not reproduce. A fifth fresh-seed run, at thirty-seven gNBs on 20 seeds, held all three of its checks, as Section~\ref{sec:results-m6} reports.

Third, a script written independently of the analysis pipeline, with its own log reader and Holm correction, recomputes from the raw logs the numbers in every results table and the key results sentences, and all 666 of its checks pass.

\subsection{Federation, Privacy, Disruption, and Live Deployment}
\label{sec:results-m3}
\label{sec:results-m4}
\label{sec:results-m8}
Three further campaigns bound AXIOM's wider claims, and Sections S9 and S12 of Online Resource 1 report them in full. In federated training each gNB trains its own copy of AXIOM on its own requests, and the copies are averaged periodically. Pooled over all 30 federated seeds, including ten fresh ones, the cost is $+0.491$ per step with 95\% CI [$-$0.08, 1.09] and $p=0.14$, so federation costs little on average, but a cost of up to about one reward per step cannot be excluded. Adding DP-SGD noise to every step lowers reward by 0.77 to 1.25 per step, and no drop survives Holm correction at ten seeds. The formal guarantee is weak, with $\varepsilon$ of about 2{,}600 or more, so per-step DP-SGD delivers no meaningful guarantee here.

Under gNB dropout and agent churn every policy loses reward significantly, and no gap between AXIOM and any baseline survives Holm correction at ten seeds. Demand spikes leave AXIOM's decisions largely intact, with block precision at 0.98, and Section S12 of Online Resource 1 gives the protocol.

A live single-gNB OAI testbed confirmed the control path that AXIOM would use, with 0.000\% loss at one and at two UEs per slice. It never ran short of capacity, so it cannot show admission under contention, and AXIOM itself is evaluated offline, as Section S9 of Online Resource 1 reports.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
Overall, no learned baseline earns significantly more reward than AXIOM in any setting we tested, and its margin over the cheaper MAPPO is largest under time-varying load, coupling, and uneven per-gNB load. Against hand-built rules, however, the picture is much narrower. A rule known in advance matches or beats AXIOM in steady conditions at every size, and AXIOM beats a tuned rule only when the load moves beyond the rule's tuning conditions. Its practical case is for operators who cannot hand-tune and re-tune rules as conditions change, rather than for a fixed, well-understood cluster.

Several limitations bound the findings reported in this paper. First, both simulators are synthetic, with a first-order coupling model, no radio channel, no user mobility, and no measured traffic traces, and the live testbed never reached contention. External validity on hardware under load therefore remains the main open question. Second, we did not reimplement a published slice-admission algorithm, so our learned baselines are standard multi-agent methods and ablations of AXIOM rather than prior admission controllers. Third, attention added nothing over a per-node encoder, and a value-decomposition baseline matched AXIOM at three gNBs, so AXIOM's contribution is a design that scales rather than a new learning principle. Fourth, charging the SLA penalty per violating gNB, so that it grows with the cluster, changed neither AXIOM's compliance nor its standing against the static rule. Finally, the pre-registrations were committed to the authors' own version-control history rather than an external registry, and development work that changed no reported result is disclosed in Section S13 of Online Resource 1.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller with a shared graph-attention encoder and per-slice heads, evaluated under a protocol designed to make its results trustworthy.

AXIOM's advantage over learned controllers grows with the cluster. Under static load it reaches 98.9\% of an oracle rule's reward at three gNBs and keeps priority-slice compliance between 93.7\% and 97.3\% from seven to thirty-seven gNBs, while every learned baseline stays below 84\%. At thirty-seven gNBs its lead over the best learned baseline reaches 35.0 points, and 36.5 points on 20 fresh seeds, with 7,782 parameters against up to 682,058. There it serves 99.8\% of URLLC demand at a mean URLLC delay of 0.08 control periods, against 0.58 to 3.61 for the learned baselines. Tuned DQN baselines stay 29.9 to 48.1 points behind it, and a value-decomposition baseline that ties it at three gNBs trails by 13.6 points at nineteen.

Under time-varying load and coupling AXIOM earns 27\% to 36\% more reward than MAPPO at three and nineteen gNBs, so its extra cost pays most where the environment is dynamic. When load rises 10\% to 20\% above the conditions a tuned rule was set for, AXIOM overtakes that rule at nineteen gNBs by 6.2 to 8.7 reward per step without retraining. Simple rules still set the bar in other settings. A static rule known in advance does better under steady load and at nineteen gNBs under coupling, and a tuned rule beats every learned policy at its tuning conditions, as Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses. The main findings held on fresh seeds never used before.

Overall, the evaluation safeguards show that learned admission control should be judged against trivial and tuned rules, with metrics tied to the objective, on independent seeds, under a pre-registered analysis.

Three directions for future work follow from these limits. First, a live multi-gNB deployment of AXIOM would test its gains under real contention. Second, giving the learner the queue-threshold structure that the tuned rules exploit may close the remaining gap to them. Finally, adding noise to the per-round model updates rather than to every step would make the formal privacy guarantee meaningful.
