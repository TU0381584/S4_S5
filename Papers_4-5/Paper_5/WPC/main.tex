\title[AXIOM for Federated Slice Admission Control]{AXIOM: A Correctness-Aware Graph-Attention Architecture for Federated Multi-Agent Slice Admission Control}

\author[1]{\fnm{Manoj Prasad} \sur{Kunasegran}}\nomail

\author*[1]{\fnm{Wai Leong} \sur{Pang}}\email{waileong.pang@taylors.edu.my}

\author[1]{\fnm{Swee King} \sur{Phang}}\nomail

\affil[1]{\orgdiv{School of Engineering}, \orgname{Taylor's University}, \orgaddress{\city{Subang Jaya}, \country{Malaysia}}}

\abstract{Fifth generation (5G) network slicing must admit heterogeneous service requests across many cooperating base stations under strict per-slice guarantees. This paper presents AXIOM, a graph-attention architecture for multi-agent slice admission control, together with the evaluation safeguards needed to trust what it reports. A shared Graph Attention Network (GAT) encoder reads the base-station cluster graph and feeds decentralised per-slice action heads, so each gNodeB (gNB) trains on a cluster-wide view yet decides locally. In a shared-budget simulator where admitting low-priority traffic starves the priority slices, AXIOM is the only learned policy that matches an oracle rule, exactly so in 35 of 40 training seeds. At three gNBs it reaches 99.6\% of the oracle's reward, against 35.6\% for a flattened single-agent baseline and 15.5\% for independent per-gNB learners. It also keeps Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) and enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) Service Level Agreement (SLA) compliance at 91.9\% and 95.5\%, against at most 61.6\% for either baseline. At 19 gNBs AXIOM still keeps priority-slice compliance at 69.8\%, while both baselines fall to 4.0\% or less and the single-agent baseline collapses into accepting every request in 43 of 45 runs. AXIOM's own collapse is rare, at 6 of 130 runs, and shows as a policy that never blocks, which makes it simple to screen for. Federated training adds no significant reward cost, whereas per-step differential privacy erodes blocking accuracy and gives only a weak formal guarantee, which we report as open. A trivial-policy check, per-slice correctness metrics, and an independent-seed replication caught four evaluation faults, including an earlier simulator in which accepting every request was optimal. A live OpenAirInterface (OAI) testbed validates the control path AXIOM would use on a real Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) stack, while AXIOM itself is evaluated offline.}

\keywords{Network Slicing, Slice Admission Control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Graph Attention Networks, Federated Learning, Open RAN}

\section{Introduction}

Network slicing virtualises the physical radio access network (RAN) to serve heterogeneous services under Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC) categories. Each slice carries distinct Service Level Agreement (SLA) targets that are in turn based on Quality of Service (QoS) thresholds. Our earlier work~\cite{mecon-paper1,icraie-paper2} validated that a Deep Q-Network (DQN) admission controller can enforce SLAs for a single gNodeB (gNB) in a live OpenAirInterface (OAI) testbed. However, a real deployment spans multiple gNBs that share backhaul, spectrum planning, and a cluster-wide load-balance objective. Thus, slice admission at one gNB is rarely isolated, and coordinated admission is in fact a multi-gNB, multi-agent challenge. This research aims to address whether a shared, graph-structured view of the cluster could enable per-gNB agents to decide better than a single agent on a flattened state, as opposed to independent agents. Fig.~\ref{fig:sysview} places the research premise in context.

This paper introduces AXIOM, an Attention-based eXecution with Integrity for O-RAN Multi-agent slice-admission controller. AXIOM is a centralised-training, decentralised-execution multi-agent architecture that is built on a shared Graph Attention Network (GAT)~\cite{gat} encoder over a multi-gNB topology, which feeds decentralised per-slice Q-heads. We deliver it together with the evaluation discipline needed to trust what it reports. Three gaps from our systematic review of 67 core studies motivate this pairing, as Section~\ref{sec:litreview} details. First, no reviewed study checks whether its own outcome metric can score a degenerate policy as well as a correct one. Second, none replicates a headline architectural finding on an independent seed sample. Third, none tests whether a live testbed can reach the contention regime its offline claims assume. We close all three gaps through a per-component realisation of the architecture, namely the GAT encoder, the Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) controller, the Federated Learning (FL) coordinator, and the Differential Privacy (DP) layer, each measured and isolated rather than folded into one end-to-end number. We apply a correctness-aware metric pair and a reproduction-versus-replication discipline throughout, and we add a live single-gNB deployment that tests, rather than assumes, whether the rig reaches genuine per-slice scarcity.

We extend the architecture to federated training with differential privacy, and we stress it under mid-episode gNB dropout, demand spikes, agent churn, and cluster growth to $N \in \{7, 19\}$ gNBs under fully-connected, ring, and hex-grid adjacency. AXIOM matches an oracle rule at three gNBs, where both DQN baselines reach at most 35.6\% of the oracle's reward, and at nineteen gNBs it is the only policy that still protects the priority slices. We report its weaknesses alongside these gains, namely a rare training collapse, weaker results at seven fully-connected gNBs, an ablation in which a plain per-node encoder matches graph attention, and a per-step DP layer that does not yet give a useful guarantee.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:litreview}
Our prior systematic review~\cite{ref:access} screened 6{,}286 records down to 67 core studies from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slicing. The review organised them around three enabling pillars, namely topology-aware graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federation. Each pillar is well developed on its own, but the three are rarely combined under realistic constraints, and never with a validated live deployment. No reviewed study checks whether its own evaluation metric can mask a degenerate policy, and none replicates a headline finding on an independent seed sample, which is the gap this paper fills.

AXIOM also sits inside the broader Open RAN (O-RAN) effort to run learned control loops as applications over standard interfaces. Polese et al.~\cite{polese2023oran} survey that architecture and its open problems, while ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran} and Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2023xapps} train and compare deep reinforcement learning controllers for slicing on large experimental platforms. Learned slice admission predates O-RAN, from market-driven admission of slice requests~\cite{bega2020market} to reinforcement learning for RAN slicing~\cite{raza2019slicing}. For cooperating agents, value factorisation such as QMIX~\cite{rashid2020qmix} and centralised-critic policy gradients such as MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} are the standard training schemes. AXIOM instead shares one graph encoder across agents and trains every pending request as its own temporal-difference sample, for the reason Section~\ref{sec:methodology-perslice} gives.

Several recent works come close on a single axis of this problem. Shao et al.~\cite{shao2021gatmarl} motivate our GAT encoder choice by showing that graph attention strengthens inter-agent cooperation over plain DQN and Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) baselines. Ahmadi et al.~\cite{ahmadi2024generalizable} study topology generalisation through graph attention, which complements but does not answer the fixed-topology evaluation-integrity question we ask in Section~\ref{sec:results-m6}. Sulaiman et al.~\cite{sulaiman2023coordinated} are the closest prior art to our joint slicing-and-admission-control problem. None of these three checks whether its reward-optimal policy could be mistaken for a degenerate one, and none replicates a headline result on an independent seed sample.

Robustness studies share our concern but stop short of it. Tashman and Cherkaoui~\cite{tashman2026adversarial} and Fatehi et al.~\cite{fatehi2026attentionmarl} study robustness to a disruptive condition, as our disruption campaign in Section~\ref{sec:results-m4} does, yet neither checks whether the reported robustness metric is trustworthy and neither replicates a finding on an independent sample. That is the gap our independent-seed replication closes, and the need is concrete, since replication overturned an early churn-immunity reading in our earlier simulator. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022federatedoran} are the closest periodic-synchronisation precedent, and Yasin et al.~\cite{yasin2025dpfl} are the closest precedent for federated learning with differential privacy. Their threat model differs from ours, since they secure the controller rather than characterise a control policy's own privacy-utility trade-off, which our $\sigma$ sweep in Section~\ref{sec:results-m3} reports, and neither adds a correctness-aware check on whether federating or privatising changed decision quality. A further, closely related set~\cite{mohajer2026pronto,bouroudi2025gnnmarl,qasim2025tgnnfl,gong2026femaddpg} combines two of the three pillars, yet like every work above, each evaluates on its own reported outcome metric, computed once, on one seed or run.

Rather than adding a new point in the GAT, MARL, and FL design space, this paper fills that gap with a demonstration on AXIOM, extended to federation, privacy, and disruption. It shows that two checks are both necessary, namely testing a metric's own trustworthiness and replicating a finding on an independent sample. Skipping either changes what a reader may believe the resulting numbers mean.

We consider a cluster of $N{=}3$ gNBs, each serving the same three slices, URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC, under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block (PRB) budget $B$. An admission decision is a PRB-ceiling adjustment through the \texttt{slicing\_control\_m} minimum and maximum ratio, rather than a literal per-flow accept or reject, since the real OAI xApp control interface exposes only ratio ceilings. No real multi-gNB physical topology is available to us, so we take the least assumption-laden choice and treat the cluster as fully connected, as a modelling choice rather than a measured fact. At each control step, every gNB holds zero or more pending admission requests, each tagged with a slice identity, and an agent decides whether to accept or reject for each request independently, conditioned on the current cluster state. The discrete action space matches our single-gNB work exactly at \texttt{action\_dim}${=}2$, so any coordination benefit found here is due to the architecture and not a richer action space.

The step reward follows our prior single-gNB formulation. It has a per-slice service term net of SLA-violation penalties, a congestion charge on admitted volume, and a load-imbalance penalty across gNBs. The step reward $r_t$ sums over slices indexed by $k$. It uses priority weight $\omega_k$, per-accept reward $R_k$, admitted count $n_k$, violation penalty $\lambda_k$, and the indicator $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ equal to one exactly when slice $k$ is in SLA violation that step,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \sum_{k}\!\big(\omega_k R_k\, n_k - \lambda_k\,\mathbf{1}[v_k]\big)
      \;-\; \kappa\, C_t \lVert a_t \rVert_1
      \;-\; \beta\,(1 - F_t),
\end{equation}
where $\lVert a_t \rVert_1$ is the total admitted this step, $C_t$ the cluster-mean congestion level, $\kappa$ the congestion coefficient, $F_t\!\in\![0,1]$ the min-over-max PRB-utilisation fairness ratio, and $\beta$ the load-balance weight. The violation flag $v_k$ follows from a continuous SLA margin $m_k$. The correctness-aware metrics of Section~\ref{sec:collapse} also build on this margin. We take it as the worse of the queue and loss channels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin}
m_k = \min\!\Big(1 - \tilde q_k,\; 1 - \tfrac{\ell_k}{\ell_k^{\max}}\Big),
\qquad v_k \Leftrightarrow m_k \le 0,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde q_k$ the slice backlog relative to its budget, and $\ell_k$ and $\ell_k^{\max}$ its loss proxy and loss budget. The weights are set so that, under congestion, the intended policy is differentiated shedding, which keeps accepting URLLC and eMBB and sheds mMTC.

Every result uses an offline, closed-loop simulation of the cluster, except the bounded live anchor of Section~\ref{sec:results-m8}. Each gNB has a budget of $B{=}100$ PRBs shared by its three slices. Each slice's mean offered demand is 1.05 times its nominal PRB share of 30, 60, and 10 PRBs for URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC. Aggregate demand therefore exceeds the budget by about five per cent, while URLLC and eMBB demand stays below their own caps of 33 and 66 PRBs. Whenever the slices' admitted demand exceeds $B$, they share $B$ in proportion to that demand, so every admitted mMTC request takes resources from URLLC and eMBB. Unserved demand carries forward as backlog within an episode, which drives the SLA margin of Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin}, and every episode starts with empty queues, and per-gNB load multipliers drawn from $[0.9, 1.0]$ keep the cluster heterogeneous. This shared-budget design replaces an earlier simulator that served each slice independently at about a quarter of the budget, where accepting every request was harmless. Section~\ref{sec:collapse} shows how a trivial accept-all baseline exposed that, and verifies that shedding mMTC is both the reward-optimal and the SLA-protecting behaviour in the current design. The concrete rig behind every live measurement is the single-host, software-radio, O-RAN stack of Fig.~\ref{fig:testbed}, which runs without a near-real-time RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC). We omit the RIC because the ORANSlice fork applies slicing control directly through a lightweight UDP E2 agent, which exercises the real per-slice PRB-ratio primitive without the overhead of a separate RIC on a single commodity host.

\section{The AXIOM Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}

AXIOM differs from its two baselines in exactly what each policy sees and shares. AXIOM runs one graph-attention encoder over the whole gNB cluster, trains that encoder jointly from every agent's experience, and reads out one dedicated Q-head per slice, so every gNB decides from a shared, topology-aware view of the cluster. The single-agent DQN keeps one controller over a flattened joint state, with no per-gNB structure and no graph. The independent DQN gives each gNB its own learner that sees only local features and never shares parameters or reads the cluster topology. The two baselines are therefore ablations of AXIOM that remove the shared encoder and the graph input together, so any gap between them and AXIOM measures what those components add jointly. A third variant that keeps everything except attention isolates the graph's own contribution in Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}.

\subsection{Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads}
\label{sec:methodology-perslice}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}a shows the architecture. At every control step, each gNB forms its own per-slice feature row $x_i$. The three features are PRB-used-ratio, congestion-level, and queue-length-norm. These rows stack into a joint node-feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ over the $N$ gNB nodes, with $d$ features each. A two-layer, $H{=}4$-head GAT~\cite{gat} produces one embedding per gNB. Each head $h$ decomposes additive attention rather than scoring a concatenation. It uses a learned source vector $a_s^{h}$ and destination vector $a_d^{h}$, applied to the shared linear projection $W^{h}$. Here $\phi$ is the leaky rectifier of slope $0.2$, and $\mathcal{N}_i$ is the set of neighbours of node $i$ under the adjacency mask,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat}
\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}=\phi\!\big(a_s^{h\top} W^{h} x_i + a_d^{h\top} W^{h} x_j\big),
\qquad
\alpha_{ij}^{h}=\frac{\exp\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}}{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{N}_i}\exp\varepsilon_{il}^{h}}.
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon_{ij}^{h}$ is the unnormalised attention score from node $i$ to neighbour $j$ in head $h$, and $\alpha_{ij}^{h}$ is its normalisation over $i$'s neighbours, with $l$ ranging over $\mathcal{N}_i$. Hidden layers concatenate the $H$ heads under an ELU nonlinearity, while the final layer averages them. A per-layer normalisation operator $\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)$ then rescales each node's embedding to zero mean and unit variance, and produces the final per-gNB embedding $z_i$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gat-agg}
z_i=\mathrm{LN}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{H}\textstyle\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_i}\alpha_{ij}^{h}\,W^{h} x_j\Big).
\end{equation}
Centralisation comes from this encoder, whose parameters every agent's loss updates jointly, so it sees the full cluster state during training. Decentralised execution comes next, since at action-selection time each agent consumes only its own embedding row $z_i$ to decide its own pending requests.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{AXIOM: centralised training, decentralised execution}
\label{alg:gat-ctde}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE encoder $f_\theta$, per-slice Q-heads $\{Q_{\phi_k}\}$, targets $f_{\theta^-},\{Q_{\phi_k^-}\}$, buffer $\mathcal{D}$, adjacency $A$, discount $\gamma$, sync period $C$
\FOR{each step $t$, observing joint node features $X_t$}
  \STATE $E_t \gets f_\theta(X_t, A)$ \COMMENT{centralised, one row per gNB}
  \STATE for each pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$, pick $a \gets \arg\max_a Q_{\phi_k}(E_t[i])[a]$, $\epsilon$-greedy \COMMENT{decentralised, own row only}
  \STATE step env, store $(X_t, X_{t+1}, \{(i,k,a)\}, r_t, \text{done})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
  \IF{$|\mathcal{D}| \geq$ warmup}
    \STATE sample minibatch $B$; $E_B \gets f_\theta(X_B,A)$, $E'_B \gets f_{\theta^-}(X'_B,A)$
    \STATE per request, $y \gets r + \gamma(1{-}\text{done})\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}(E'_B[i])[a']$
    \STATE $\mathcal{L} \gets$ mean per-request Huber loss, never summed per step; update $\theta,\{\phi_k\}$, clip grad-norm 1.0
  \ENDIF
  \STATE every $C$ episodes, $\theta^-\gets\theta$, $\phi_k^-\gets\phi_k$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The \texttt{LayerNorm} and the per-slice heads are deliberate design choices. With a single shared Q-head, all three slices update the same parameters, and URLLC's large priority weight produces gradients that swamp mMTC's much smaller signal, a standard shared-parameter class imbalance. One small multi-layer perceptron (MLP) per slice, selected by the context one-hot, gives each slice its own parameters, and the \texttt{LayerNorm} stops an embedding from encoding congestion through its magnitude alone. A seed here is one independent random initialisation of network weights and environment stream, and we report every seed rather than a single best run.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:gat-ctde} states the training loop, where the encoder updates from every request in a sampled minibatch regardless of gNB or slice. This is centralised training, while action selection at rollout time consults only the acting agent's own embedding row, which is decentralised execution. Every pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$ is its own temporal-difference sample, and we never sum requests across a step before the loss. For a request with immediate reward $r$, next-state embedding $z_i'$, and candidate next action $a'$, the bootstrapped target $y$ at discount $\gamma$ and the minibatch loss are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td}
y = r + \gamma\,(1-d)\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}\!\big(z_i'\big)[a'],
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{(i,k,a)\in B}\ell_\delta\!\Big(Q_{\phi_k}(z_i)[a]-y\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\phi_k}$ is slice $k$'s online Q-head, $Q_{\phi_k^-}$ its target copy, $d$ the episode-done flag, and $B$ the sampled minibatch of requests. We use the Huber loss $\ell_\delta$ in place of squared error, which keeps the deeper encoder stable under long bootstraps. An earlier variant passed each gNB's summed per-request values through a QMIX-style monotonic mixer~\cite{rashid2020qmix}. Because the number of pending requests changes from step to step, that mixed target changed scale with the request count, and the mean training loss diverged, rising from about 76 over the first fifth of training steps to about 409 over the last fifth. Treating each request as its own sample removes that dependence.

\subsection{Federated Training with Differential Privacy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}b shows the federated variant. In this variant, each gNB becomes a federated-learning client. It holds a private copy of the same network and trains only on its own requests. Transitions are partitioned by owning agent, so no client sees another's raw admission data. Each client's GAT encoder still consumes the full joint node-feature snapshot. Every $R$ local steps, the server averages all clients' online weights by FedAvg~\cite{fedavg} and broadcasts the result back. We also implement FedProx~\cite{fedprox}, which adds a proximal term that pulls local weights toward the last broadcast global model. We sweep it at $\mu \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$ on three seeds. FedProx never improves on FedAvg, reaching 9.42 to 10.49 reward per step against FedAvg's 10.79 with perfect block precision throughout, so we keep FedAvg.

Differential privacy (DP) bounds how much any one client's training data can change what the shared model reveals. We apply it as a differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) step~\cite{dpsgd}. Each client clips its minibatch gradient to a fixed norm $C_{\text{dp}}$ and, when privacy is enabled, adds Gaussian noise of standard deviation $\sigma C_{\text{dp}}$ before its optimiser step. The clip applies to the whole minibatch gradient rather than to each example, so changing any of the client's data in one step moves the clipped gradient by at most $2C_{\text{dp}}$, and the guarantee covers the client's whole step batch. We account for it with zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP)~\cite{zcdp}, where $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ are the standard privacy-loss and failure-probability parameters. Composing the Gaussian mechanism over the $m$ noisy steps a client takes gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zcdp}
\rho=\frac{2m}{\sigma^{2}},\qquad
\varepsilon(\delta)=\rho+2\sqrt{\rho\ln(1/\delta)}.
\end{equation}
We credit no amplification by subsampling, because minibatches come from a growing replay buffer that reuses records, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:zcdp} is an upper bound. The clip is applied whether or not noise is added, so a $\sigma{=}0$ run and a $\sigma{>}0$ run differ only in the injected noise. The noise protects each client's own admission decisions and rewards, but not its congestion features, which every client's encoder reads from the shared node-feature snapshot by design. Each federated round therefore runs $R$ local Huber-TD steps per client with an optional FedProx proximal term $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert \theta_i - \bar\theta \rVert^2$, clips and optionally perturbs the gradient, and closes with a FedAvg average that is broadcast back.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Every campaign uses 300 training and 50 evaluation episodes per seed, and each seed is evaluated on a held-out environment stream. The centralised campaign uses 30 seeds, 900 to 929, and compares AXIOM with two learned baselines and one rule. The single-agent DQN is one controller over the flattened joint state. The independent DQN gives each gNB its own DQN with no parameter sharing and no topology information. An ablation is a deliberately reduced variant with one component removed, so its effect can be isolated, and both DQN policies are ablations in that sense.

The oracle rule needs no learning, since it simply accepts every URLLC and eMBB request and sheds mMTC whenever its gNB's congestion level reaches a threshold $\tau$. We choose $\tau$ on the training-seed streams only and evaluate it on the held-out streams, and the selected value is $\tau{=}0$, so the rule sheds every mMTC request. It encodes exactly the behaviour the reward was designed to favour, so it serves as a reference ceiling rather than a competitor. Two further arms isolate AXIOM's components, namely the two DQN baselines rerun at AXIOM's learning rate, and an AXIOM variant whose graph encoder is replaced by a per-node MLP of the same width that ignores the adjacency.

All arms share the discount factor $\gamma=0.95$, the per-episode epsilon schedule, the target-synchronisation period, and the training budget. AXIOM trains at learning rate $1{\times}10^{-4}$ and the DQN baselines at $1{\times}10^{-3}$ by default, all under the Adam optimiser, with epsilon decaying from $1.0$ to $0.05$ at a per-episode factor of $0.985$ and the target network synchronised every 10 episodes. The GAT encoder uses hidden and output dimension 16, feeding per-slice Q-heads of hidden dimension 32. A sensitivity study repeats the learned arms and the oracle rule on 10 seeds under three changed conditions. Two change the load, to 1.02 and 1.07 times the nominal shares, and the third perturbs the weights, lowering eMBB's priority weight from 3.5 to 2.5 and raising mMTC's from 0.3 to 0.6.

The federated campaign uses seeds 900 to 909 and a five-level noise sweep $\sigma \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4\}$, and each federated round runs 50 local steps under FedAvg with a gradient-clip norm of $1.0$. A disjoint replication sample, seeds 1000 to 1009, repeats the headline comparisons. All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95\% bootstrap percentile intervals over 10{,}000 resamples. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and each family of comparisons against AXIOM is Holm-corrected for multiple testing.

A validation screen, fixed before the final campaign was run, checks every trained checkpoint of every learned arm before evaluation. Each checkpoint plays five greedy episodes on its own training stream, never on the held-out stream, and fails the screen if it blocks no request or if fewer than 90\% of its blocks target mMTC. We report every seed whether or not it passes, and separately report a two-seed deployment protocol that trains a pair of seeds and deploys the first one to pass. The same screen and protocol apply to every learned arm, so they give AXIOM no advantage the baselines lack.

\subsection{Evaluation Safeguards}
\label{sec:collapse}
Three safeguards make the results below trustworthy, and Table~\ref{tab:safeguards} lists the failure each one caught during this work. A standard pipeline would have reported every one of them as a clean result.

The first is a trivial-policy check, in which we evaluate the simplest rules the environment admits before trusting any learned policy, namely accepting every request and shedding one slice outright. In the first version of our simulator, every slice was served independently against its own ceiling at about a quarter of the gNB's budget. There, accepting every request earned 14.45 reward per step and kept URLLC and eMBB at 100\% SLA compliance, more than any learned policy, so admission control had nothing to do and policies that appeared to fail by never blocking were in fact optimal. The shared-budget simulator of Section~\ref{sec:system-model} removes that loophole. Over the same 30 held-out seeds, accepting every request now earns $-4.05$ reward per step and drops URLLC and eMBB compliance to 2.4\% and 1.1\%, whereas shedding mMTC earns 10.94 and keeps them at 92.2\% and 95.6\%. Every result in this paper comes from the shared-budget simulator. Its feasible range has an edge, since at 1.07 times the nominal shares even shedding every mMTC request leaves URLLC and eMBB compliance below 7\%.

\label{sec:correctness-metrics}
The second is a correctness-aware set of metrics, computed from data every run already logs. Mean reward per step is the training objective. Block precision is the fraction of blocked requests that correctly target mMTC, the slice the reward calibration makes reject-optimal. Writing $b_{\text{mMTC}}$ and $b$ for the mMTC-targeted and total blocks over an evaluation, it is $\pi = b_{\text{mMTC}}/b$ for $b>0$ and undefined for $b=0$, so a seed that never blocks is counted as a collapse rather than scored as a success. Per-slice SLA compliance then shows whether the shedding actually protects URLLC and eMBB. We do not use the common all-slice compliance metric, because correct shedding gives up mMTC's own SLA by design, so that metric scores a correct policy and a failed one alike.

The third is a reproduction-versus-replication discipline, in which we recompute every headline comparison on an independent, disjoint seed sample and report both. Reproduction re-runs the same seeds, whereas replication draws fresh ones, so only replication can catch a result that is an artifact of one seed sample. It caught one such artifact in this work. A weaker replication led us to inspect step-level traces, which showed that queue backlog carried over between episodes. With only about half a PRB of headroom once mMTC is shed, a backlog built by one early transient never drained, so a single bad episode spoiled every later one for every policy, the oracle rule included. Every result in this paper comes from the corrected simulator, which empties all queues at the start of each episode.

\subsection{Centralised}
\label{sec:results-m2}

The three-gNB fully-connected setting is deliberately the hardest case for a graph encoder, since every gNB already sees every other and there is little topology left to exploit. Section~\ref{sec:results-m6} tests the encoder again once the cluster grows and its adjacency thins.

Table~\ref{tab:m2-results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:m2-results} report the thirty-seed campaign. AXIOM learns the intended policy in every seed. It never collapses, every one of its blocks lands on mMTC, and it keeps URLLC and eMBB at 91.9\% and 95.5\% SLA compliance. Its mean reward of 10.892 per step reaches 99.6\% of the oracle rule's 10.935, and the two tie outright in 28 of the 30 seeds, so AXIOM recovers the reward-optimal behaviour from the reward alone, without being told which slice to shed. The two DQN baselines fall far short, with the single-agent DQN reaching 35.6\% of the oracle reward and the independent DQN 15.5\%. Both let URLLC and eMBB compliance fall to between 25.5\% and 61.6\%, because they also turn away some URLLC and eMBB requests and shed mMTC less consistently. AXIOM leads the single-agent DQN by $+6.997$ per step, with 95\% CI [5.01, 9.12], and the independent DQN by $+9.199$, with 95\% CI [7.21, 11.10], both at Holm-adjusted $p<0.0001$. It wins or ties in all 30 seeds against the single-agent DQN and in 29 of 30 against the independent DQN.

A disjoint replication on seeds 1000 to 1009 tests whether this result depends on the seed sample, and it is weaker. These seeds are harder, since even the oracle rule earns 10.290 rather than 10.935. AXIOM matches the oracle exactly in 7 of the 10 seeds, while in the other three it learns a partial policy that either blocks some URLLC and eMBB requests or sheds too little mMTC. Its leads of $+5.419$ over the single-agent DQN and $+2.867$ over the independent DQN favour AXIOM in 8 of 10 seeds each, but at ten seeds they reach only Holm-adjusted $p=0.059$ and $p=0.21$. Pooled over all 40 seeds, AXIOM reaches 97.1\% of the oracle reward and leads the two baselines by $+6.602$ and $+7.616$ per step, winning 36 of 40 seeds against each at Holm-adjusted $p<0.0001$. It falls short of the oracle by 0.309 per step, a small but significant gap at $p=0.043$.

\subsection{Baselines, Ablations, and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results-ablation}
Three checks test where AXIOM's advantage comes from. First, the learning rate does not explain it. Rerun at AXIOM's rate of $10^{-4}$, the single-agent DQN reaches 4.271 and the independent DQN 3.436 reward per step, and AXIOM still leads them by $+6.621$ and $+7.457$ at Holm-adjusted $p<0.0001$.

Second, at three fully-connected gNBs the graph encoder adds nothing measurable. Replacing it with a per-node MLP of the same width lowers reward only from 10.892 to 10.723, a difference of $+0.169$ with 95\% CI [$-$0.04, 0.50] and $p=0.25$. This is expected, since each gNB's own congestion carries most of this decision, so at this scale the gain comes from the shared parameters, the per-slice heads, and the joint training rather than from attention. Section~\ref{sec:results-m6} repeats this test at nineteen gNBs, with the same outcome.

Third, the advantage holds when the environment changes within its feasible range, and AXIOM tracks the oracle rule under every change, tying it in 7 to 9 of 10 seeds with no significant difference. With demand lowered to 1.02 times the nominal shares, AXIOM keeps URLLC and eMBB at 100\% and leads the single-agent and independent DQN by $+1.880$ and $+3.922$ reward per step. With eMBB's weight lowered to 2.5 and mMTC's raised to 0.6, it keeps both priority slices above 95\% while the baselines let eMBB compliance fall to 1.5\% or less, and its lead widens to $+5.599$ and $+9.045$. All four leads hold at Holm-adjusted $p<0.02$ over 10 seeds. At 1.07 times the nominal shares the environment passes its feasibility edge, since even the oracle rule leaves URLLC and eMBB below 7\%. There AXIOM matches the oracle, and the single-agent DQN, which also turns away some URLLC and eMBB traffic, keeps more URLLC at 26.4\% against 5.7\%, with no significant reward difference. Protecting URLLC beyond that edge needs a policy that also sheds some eMBB, which this reward calibration does not encourage.

With federation switched on and no noise, mean reward per step on the ten shared seeds moves from 11.333 for centralised AXIOM to 10.481 for the federated variant, a difference of $+0.853$ with 95\% CI [$-$0.005, 2.431] and $p=0.31$. The DQN baselines are not federated, because averaging the independent learners' weights would turn them into a parameter-sharing learner that is no longer the ablation, and the single-agent DQN is one central controller with nothing to federate.

Adding DP-SGD noise to every step is a different matter. At every tested noise multiplier, from $\sigma{=}0.5$ to $\sigma{=}4$, block precision falls to between 0.27 and 0.49, eMBB compliance falls to at most 20.3\%, and reward drops to between 3.5 and 5.5 per step, all significantly below centralised AXIOM at Holm-adjusted $p<0.02$, as Fig.~\ref{fig:m3-privacy}b shows. The formal guarantee is also weak at these settings. Each client takes 17{,}969 noisy steps, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:zcdp} gives $\varepsilon$ of about 146{,}000, 37{,}000, 9{,}600, and 2{,}600 at $\delta{=}10^{-5}$ for $\sigma$ of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4, far from the single-digit values that carry meaning. Per-step DP-SGD therefore delivers neither a usable policy nor a meaningful guarantee here. A useful guarantee would need noise on the roughly 360 per-round model updates rather than on every step, which Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} lists as future work. Read together, the federation result answers the deployability question, since periodic, infrequent synchronisation matches centralised training without per-step coordination between gNBs, while privacy remains open.

\subsection{Disruption}
\label{sec:results-m4}
We test every policy's frozen checkpoint against three mid-episode disruptions, injected at evaluation time with no retraining. A 'gNB dropout' forces one gNB to reject everything and blanks its row in the shared feature matrix for a window. A 'demand spike' briefly multiplies the arrival rate from outside the policy. An 'agent churn' swaps one gNB's decisions for a fresh, untrained copy of its own policy for a window and leaves the others intact. Churn applies to every genuinely multi-agent policy but not to the single-agent one. For dropout and churn the severity is how long the window lasts, at 10\%, 30\%, or 60\% of the episode. For the spike it is the arrival multiplier, at 2$\times$, 4$\times$, or 8$\times$. Because raw reward grows mechanically with request volume, we report a volume-normalised reward over an episode of $T$ steps, $\bar r = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t/\max(n_t,1)$, which divides each step reward $r_t$ by its own pending-request count $n_t$ before averaging.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_m4_disruption.pdf}
\caption{Disruption campaign, 10 seeds per arm, 95\% CIs. a, b, Normalised
reward kept under dropout and churn, with the undisrupted level at None.
c, Block precision under demand spikes.}
\label{fig:m4-disruption}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:m4-disruption} plots the reward each policy keeps rather than the reward it loses, because AXIOM starts from a far higher level. Undisrupted, AXIOM earns 3.25 normalised reward, against 0.62 for the single-agent DQN and 0.43 for the independent DQN, so the same disruption can cost it more while still leaving it ahead. Dropout and churn hurt every policy significantly at every severity, in 8 to 10 of 10 seeds at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.041$. The cost rises steeply from the 10\% to the 30\% window and then levels off.

Dropout is the one disruption that erases AXIOM's lead. A dropped gNB rejects every request, including URLLC and eMBB, so its penalties dominate the episode whatever the policy does. At the 10\% window AXIOM still keeps 0.49, against $-0.54$ for both baselines, a lead that does not survive correction, and at 30\% and 60\% all three policies end between $-1.67$ and $-2.22$ with no significant difference. Churn is different, since AXIOM keeps 1.57, 0.50, and 0.29 across the three windows while the independent DQN falls to $-0.47$, $-1.31$, and $-1.51$. AXIOM keeps more reward than the independent DQN in 8 or 9 of 10 seeds at every churn severity, by 1.8 to 2.0, although none of these gaps survives Holm correction across all 24 comparisons at ten seeds.

Demand spikes leave AXIOM's admission decisions essentially intact. Its block precision stays between 0.99 and 1.00 at every multiplier, against about 0.96 for the independent DQN and 0.90 for the single-agent DQN, as Fig.~\ref{fig:m4-disruption}c shows. AXIOM keeps more reward than either baseline under every spike, in all 10 seeds, at Holm-adjusted $p=0.047$. Its reward rises slightly during a spike, which is a counting effect of the extra requests rather than a better policy. Federated AXIOM follows centralised AXIOM closely under all three disruptions, with no significant difference at any severity.

A disjoint replication on seeds 1000 to 1009 reproduces this pattern for dropout and churn. Both again hurt every policy at every severity, at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.035$, and at the 30\% and 60\% dropout windows all policies again end within 0.4 of each other. At the 10\% dropout window AXIOM's lead over the independent DQN, 1.49, reaches significance in this sample at $p=0.039$. Under churn AXIOM again keeps more reward than the independent DQN in 7 to 9 of 10 seeds, by about 1.15, without reaching significance. One small difference appears only in the replication, where federated AXIOM keeps 0.35 to 0.37 more reward than centralised AXIOM at the two longer dropout windows, at $p=0.023$.

Replication first proved its worth in this campaign, on the earlier simulator. There, a ten-seed sample suggested that the independent DQN was immune to churn, since it never reads another agent's features, and a disjoint replication overturned that reading with a significant cost at every severity. In the shared-budget environment the primary sample already shows that churn hurts every policy, so the corrected reading holds.

\subsection{Cluster-Size Scaling}
\label{sec:results-m6}
We grow the cluster from three gNBs to seven and nineteen under three layouts, fully-connected, ring, and hex-grid. Every gNB keeps its own shared 100-PRB budget and its own demand, so per-gNB load stays the same as the cluster grows. Each of the 18 cells, one per size, layout, and policy, runs 15 seeds, namely 900 to 911 and the replication seeds 1000 to 1002. Only AXIOM's encoder reads the layout, so the independent DQN gives identical results under all three layouts, which serves as a check that the layout changes nothing else. The single-agent DQN also ignores the layout, yet its results vary between layouts in some seeds, because its training is not bit-reproducible. Rerunning one of its seeds under an unchanged layout moved its reward by up to 4.8 per step. AXIOM and the independent DQN, by contrast, reproduce exactly on rerun. We test the per-layout gaps on reward and on the mean of URLLC and eMBB compliance, Holm-correcting the 12 comparisons at each size.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig6_m6_topology.pdf}
\caption{Scaling, 15 seeds per cell. a, Collapse rate pooled over layouts,
with collapsed over total cells. b, Mean URLLC and eMBB compliance at 19
gNBs by layout, per-seed points behind the mean and 95\% CI.}
\label{fig:m6-topology}
\end{figure}

At nineteen gNBs the gap between AXIOM and both baselines is the widest in this paper. Pooled over the three layouts, AXIOM keeps URLLC and eMBB at 73.9\% and 65.8\% compliance. The independent DQN keeps them at 6.4\% and 1.5\%, and the single-agent DQN at 1.5\% and 0.9\%. Fig.~\ref{fig:m6-topology}a shows why the single-agent DQN fails, since it collapses into accepting every request in 43 of its 45 cells. The independent DQN never collapses, yet it still sheds too little mMTC to protect the priority slices. AXIOM collapses in only 2 of its 45 cells. Under every layout AXIOM leads both baselines on priority-slice compliance by 63 to 71 percentage points, winning 12 to 15 of 15 seeds, and on cluster reward by 13.1 to 22.9 per step, all at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.009$.

At seven gNBs the picture is mixed and we report it in full. AXIOM still protects the priority slices best, at 77.9\% URLLC and 77.0\% eMBB compliance, against 55.8\% and 10.4\% for the single-agent DQN and 27.7\% and 3.9\% for the independent DQN. Under the ring layout it leads both baselines on compliance and reward at Holm-adjusted $p\leq0.032$. Under the hex layout its compliance lead is significant against both baselines, but neither reward lead survives correction. Under the fully-connected layout only the compliance lead over the independent DQN is significant, at $p=0.005$. AXIOM is also the only policy that collapses at this size, in 4 of 45 cells, against none for either baseline. The weakest results come from the fully-connected layout, where every gNB already sees every other.

An ablation tests whether graph attention drives AXIOM's advantage at scale. We retrain AXIOM at nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, seeds 900 to 911, with its encoder replaced by the per-node MLP of Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}, which ignores the layout. The MLP variant does at least as well, matching AXIOM exactly in 6 of 12 seeds and exceeding it in 5, with mean URLLC and eMBB compliance of 88.5\% against 63.9\%. The difference is not significant at $p=0.094$, and neither variant collapses. AXIOM's lead over the two baselines therefore comes from its shared parameters, per-slice heads, and joint training rather than from attention over the layout, at least for this layout and scale. In hindsight this is what the simulator allows, since each gNB has its own PRB budget and no gNB's admissions change another's capacity, so even the optimal rule needs only local information. A fair test of attention needs coupling between neighbouring cells, such as inter-cell interference along the adjacency graph, which we leave to future work.

Collapse in AXIOM is rare and concentrated in a few seeds. Across all 130 AXIOM cells, namely 40 at three gNBs including the replication, 45 at seven, and 45 at nineteen, it collapses in 6, from only four distinct training seeds, and one seed accounts for three of the six. A collapsed policy never blocks, so a validation rollout on training streams should reveal it before deployment. This suggests a simple protocol, in which an operator trains two seeds and deploys one that blocks. If collapses are independent across seeds at the observed rate of 6 in 90 at seven and nineteen gNBs, both seeds fail with probability of about 0.4\%. We have not tested this protocol, so we state it as a mitigation rather than a result.

AXIOM alone reaches the upper-right region, at 99.6\% of the oracle reward and 69.8\% compliance with 95\% CI [58.7\%, 80.6\%]. \subsection{Live Deployment}
\label{sec:results-m8}
This section reports what the real OAI testbed of Fig.~\ref{fig:testbed}, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:system-model}, can and cannot establish about the offline-trained policy. AXIOM itself is not deployed on the live testbed. Its multi-gNB policy needs at least two live gNBs, and our two-gNB attempt registered both gNBs with the core but did not sustain traffic, so a live AXIOM run is left to future work. This section therefore validates the deployment pipeline that AXIOM would use, namely the E2 control path, the PRB-ratio actuation, and real-time inference of an offline-trained checkpoint. For that we run a single-agent-DQN checkpoint, seed 900, against a real gNB, real User Equipments (UEs), and real per-slice User Datagram Protocol (UDP) traffic, at one UE per slice and again at two UEs per slice.

A capacity probe at a deliberately wide-open ceiling showed all three slices serving essentially 100\% of offered demand with 0.000\% loss at both loads. The frozen checkpoint's decision logic runs correctly end to end, through the real E2 control loop, with real accept and reject decisions, and its signature low-priority-only blocking of mMTC reappears at both loads, which shows that the deployment pipeline works end to end. Because the rig never actually runs short of capacity, the same pattern is equally consistent with the policy's trained priority bias, so we claim admission-control behaviour under contention only in the offline shared-budget environment, not live. Two integrity issues surfaced and were fixed during these checks. A scheduler-configuration error had turned the per-slice PRB-ratio ranges into raw grants below the gNB scheduler's hard five-PRB floor, which we fixed at the root, after which direct instrumentation confirmed zero below-floor grants across more than 100{,}000 readings. A separate unit mismatch in the live SLA-margin diagnostic was corrected by converting backlog into a queueing-delay estimate, using Little's law, against each slice's own latency budget.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented AXIOM, a graph-attention architecture for multi-agent slice admission control, together with the evaluation safeguards needed to trust it. A shared GAT encoder reads the cluster graph and feeds decentralised per-slice action heads. The design addresses three gaps from our prior systematic review, namely trustworthy evaluation, privacy-preserving coordination, and validated deployment.

In a shared-budget environment where admitting mMTC traffic starves the priority slices, AXIOM matches an oracle rule at three gNBs. It reaches 99.6\% of the oracle's reward, while the single-agent and independent DQN baselines reach 35.6\% and 15.5\%. A disjoint replication is weaker, with AXIOM matching the oracle in 7 of 10 seeds, yet over all 40 seeds it still reaches 97.1\% of the oracle's reward. At nineteen gNBs it keeps mean URLLC and eMBB compliance near 70\%, while both baselines keep it below 5\% and the single-agent DQN collapses in almost every run. Federated training costs no significant reward against centralised training. Per-step DP-SGD, however, erodes blocking accuracy and gives a weak formal guarantee, and AXIOM itself collapses in 6 of 130 runs, a failure that shows as a policy that never blocks. An ablation shows that these gains come from the shared encoder, the per-slice heads, and joint training rather than from attention, since a per-node MLP encoder matched AXIOM at three and nineteen gNBs. These results are offline, the graph models only the gNB cluster, and the live testbed validates the control path rather than AXIOM itself.

The evaluation safeguards changed what this paper finally reports. A trivial accept-all check exposed an earlier simulator in which admission control had nothing to do, and per-slice SLA metrics separate correct shedding from collapse where all-slice compliance cannot. The broader lesson is that evaluation needs metrics tied to the objective, a check that the simplest policies fail, and an independent seed sample before any architectural claim is trusted.

Future work targets the gaps that this paper leaves open. The first is a live multi-gNB deployment of AXIOM itself, together with a simulator in which neighbouring cells interfere, so that attention over the cluster graph has real coupling to exploit. The second is a privacy layer that adds noise to the per-round model updates rather than to every step, so that the formal guarantee becomes meaningful. The third is Multi-Access Edge Computing (MEC) scaling, which adds compute-placement nodes to the cluster graph and extends the controller to joint admission and offloading. We also plan cross-operator federation and direct Quality of Experience (QoE) inference in the reward.
