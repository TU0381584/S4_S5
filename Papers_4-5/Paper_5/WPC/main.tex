\title[AXIOM for Scalable Multi-Agent 5G Slice Admission]{AXIOM: A Shared Graph-Attention Controller for Scalable Multi-Agent 5G Slice Admission}

\author{\fnm{Manoj Prasad} \sur{Kunasegran}}\nomail

\author*{\fnm{Wai Leong} \sur{Pang}\textsuperscript{*}}\email{waileong.pang@taylors.edu.my}

\author{\fnm{Swee King} \sur{Phang}}\nomail

\affil{\orgdiv{School of Engineering, Faculty of Innovation and Technology}, \orgname{Taylor's University}, \orgaddress{\city{Subang Jaya}, \postcode{47500}, \state{Selangor}, \country{Malaysia}}}

\abstract{Fifth generation (5G) network slicing must admit heterogeneous requests across cooperating base stations under strict per-slice guarantees. Multi-agent learning can adapt these decisions to changing user demand and load. However, state-of-the-art research rarely evaluates learned multi-agent controllers against trivial policies or on independent seeds. We present AXIOM, an Adaptive Cross-cell Integrity-aware Open Radio Access Network Multi-agent slice-admission controller. Its graph-attention encoder, shared by every gNodeB (gNB), feeds one decision head per slice, keeping its size fixed as clusters grow. A pre-registered protocol with both checks compares it with single-agent and independent deep Q-networks (DQN), multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), value decomposition, and tuned rules, under static and coupled time-varying load. At 7, 19, and 37 gNBs, AXIOM keeps Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) and Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) service-level compliance above 93\%, while every learned baseline stays below 84\%. At 37 gNBs, AXIOM leads the best learned baseline by 35.0 to 36.5 points. There it serves 99.8\% of URLLC demand with a backlog delay 7 to 44 times shorter than the learned baselines, using only 7,782 parameters against 64,770 and 682,058 for the DQN baselines. Under coupling, AXIOM earns 27\% to 36\% more reward than MAPPO at 3 and 19 gNBs, and 39\% more than a static shedding rule at 3 gNBs. When load rises 10\% to 20\% past a tuned rule's conditions, AXIOM overtakes that rule by up to 8.7 reward per step. AXIOM is therefore a viable alternative for dynamic, scalable, self-optimising slice admission in next-generation 5G networks.}

\keywords{Network Slicing, Slice Admission Control, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Graph Neural Networks, Federated Learning, Open RAN}

\section{Introduction}
Network slicing virtualises the Radio Access Network (RAN) to serve Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC), Enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB), and Massive Machine-Type Communication (mMTC), each under its own Service Level Agreement (SLA). Our earlier simulation studies showed that deep reinforcement learning, including a deep Q-network (DQN), can protect URLLC SLAs at a single congested gNodeB (gNB)~\cite{mecon-paper1} and can balance load across a gNB cluster~\cite{icraie-paper2}. However, those controllers used a centralised approach, whereas a real deployment spans many gNBs that share spectrum planning and a cluster-wide load-balance objective. Consequently, admission control across such a cluster becomes a multi-agent problem. In this research, we ask whether per-gNB agents that share a view of the whole cluster can decide better than a single centralised agent or independent per-gNB agents. Fig.~\ref{fig:sysview} places our research question within the broader system context.

This paper introduces AXIOM, an acronym for Adaptive Cross-cell Integrity-aware Open Radio Access Network Multi-agent slice-admission controller. Here the Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) denotes a softwarised and open-source RAN architecture, and integrity-aware means that every reported result passes checks of evaluation integrity. One encoder, shared by every gNB, feeds a separate Q-head for each slice, under centralised training and decentralised execution. The encoder is a graph attention network (GAT)~\cite{wu2021gnnsurvey}, and the per-slice heads, each a small multi-layer perceptron (MLP), carry AXIOM's advantage as the cluster grows. Three gaps found in our prior systematic review of 67 core studies motivate pairing AXIOM with a stricter evaluation, as Section~\ref{sec:litreview} details. No reviewed study checks whether its outcome metric can score a degenerate policy, such as one that accepts every request, as well as a correct one, which blocks just enough low-priority requests to protect the priority slices. In addition, none replicates a headline finding on an independent seed sample, and none tests whether a live testbed reaches the contention that its offline claims assume. A seed fixes the random draws of one run, such as its starting weights and the traffic it trains and is tested on, so a finding that also holds on seeds never used before is unlikely to be an artefact of one sample.

We evaluate AXIOM in two offline simulators of a gNB cluster. Firstly, we consider a simple environment (Env A) with a static load, where shedding every mMTC request is close to optimal. Secondly, we consider Env B, which adds time-varying load and inter-cell coupling, so shedding one fixed slice is no longer optimal. We then compare AXIOM with a single-agent deep Q-network (DQN-SA), independent per-gNB deep Q-networks (DQN-I), multi-agent proximal policy optimisation (MAPPO), and tuned threshold rules. We also grow the cluster to 7, 19, and 37 gNBs under three layouts. Further campaigns test federated training, differential privacy, and network disruption. The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item AXIOM, a slice-admission controller with a shared cluster encoder and decentralised per-slice Q-heads, whose parameter count does not grow with the cluster and whose per-slice heads, rather than attention, carry its advantage at scale.
\item Env A, a shared-budget environment with static load, built after a trivial-policy check showed that accepting every request beat every learned policy in our original simulator. In Env A, AXIOM keeps priority-slice compliance above 93\% at 7, 19, and 37 gNBs, while both DQN baselines and MAPPO stay below 84\%.
\item Env B, a more demanding environment with time-varying load and inter-cell coupling. There AXIOM leads DQN-SA, DQN-I, and MAPPO at three and nineteen gNBs and beats a static shedding rule at three, while a tuned threshold rule sets a bar that no learned policy reaches under its tuning conditions.
\item An evaluation protocol for learned slice admission control that combines trivial-policy checks, per-slice correctness metrics, independent-seed replication, tuned rule baselines, and a pre-registered analysis.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:system-model} formulates the admission problem, and Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the AXIOM architecture. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup and evaluation safeguards, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:litreview}
Our prior systematic review~\cite{ref:access} screened 6{,}286 records down to 67 core studies from 2024 to 2026 on machine-learning-driven, user-centric distributed network slicing. The review organised them around three enabling pillars, namely topology-aware graph representation, distributed multi-agent control, and privacy-preserving federation. Each pillar is well developed on its own, but they are rarely combined under realistic constraints, and never jointly validated in a live deployment.

Studies from the review's core sample show the state of each pillar. Learned admission control uses sequential twin actor-critics~\cite{ojijo2024sac} and digital-twin-accelerated deep reinforcement learning~\cite{tao2025dtac}. Multi-agent control spans sample-efficient multi-agent DQNs for inter-slice orchestration~\cite{doanis2024madqn}, scalable frameworks for dynamic RAN slicing~\cite{li2026scalablemarl}, and cooperative agents for energy-efficient slicing~\cite{phyu2025icecream}. A graph neural network with meta-learning makes bandwidth allocation scalable and transferable~\cite{hao2024hybridtask}, and Ahmadi et al.~\cite{ahmadi2024generalizable} study topology generalisation through attention. Federated deep reinforcement learning has been applied to splitting virtual network functions~\cite{amiri2024vnf} and to slice mobility~\cite{ming2024fedslice}. Two core studies combine two of the three pillars, graph attention with multi-agent learning for task offloading~\cite{mohajer2026pronto} and a graph neural network with multi-agent learning for resource orchestration~\cite{bouroudi2025gnnmarl}. Closest to our evaluation concern, Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2025pandora} compare many deep reinforcement learning agents for O-RAN in an automated pipeline, a comparison that our trivial-policy and independent-seed checks extend. None of these core studies checks its metric against a trivial policy or replicates a finding on independent seeds.

Apart from the core studies that Table~\ref{tab:related} marks, the works cited in the rest of this section lie outside the review's 67 core studies. They add earlier foundations, studies published in this journal, and further recent work. AXIOM also sits inside the broader O-RAN effort to run learned control loops over standard interfaces, which Polese et al.~\cite{polese2023oran} survey and which ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran} and Tsampazi et al.~\cite{tsampazi2023xapps} exercise on large experimental platforms. Learned slice admission predates O-RAN, from market-driven admission~\cite{bega2020market} to reinforcement learning for RAN slicing~\cite{raza2019slicing}, and Khani et al.~\cite{khani2024sacsurvey} survey it for cloud RAN. OACR$^{2}$~\cite{li2025oacr2} uses request forecasting to maximise the infrastructure provider's long-term revenue. AXIOM instead protects priority-slice SLAs, the objective of an operator bound by strict per-slice guarantees. Cooperative agents usually train by value factorisation such as QMIX~\cite{rashid2020qmix} or with a centralised critic as in MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo}, and constrained multi-agent learning has been applied to O-RAN slicing at scale~\cite{zangooei2024flexible}. Recent work in this journal applies actor-critic learning~\cite{kulkarni2024actor} and double deep Q-networks~\cite{kamal2026ddqn} to related problems. Other studies in it address delay-aware allocation~\cite{alqahtani2025dynamic}, federated learning~\cite{krishnamoorthy2026federated}, and multi-agent relay selection~\cite{sirma2025relay}. None of these studies checks its metric against a trivial policy or replicates on independent seeds. Unlike these studies, AXIOM shares one encoder across agents and gives every slice its own decision head, which Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} shows matters as the cluster grows.

Two further works come close on a single axis of this problem. Shao et al.~\cite{shao2021gatmarl} show that graph attention strengthens inter-agent cooperation. Sulaiman et al.~\cite{sulaiman2023coordinated} are the closest prior art to joint slicing and admission control, yet neither study checks for degenerate policies or replicates on independent seeds. Table~\ref{tab:related} sets AXIOM beside the six closest works from both groups, and none of them combines admission control, a graph encoder, and federated training as AXIOM does.

Robustness studies~\cite{tashman2026adversarial,fatehi2026attentionmarl} test disruptive conditions, as our disruption campaign does, yet neither checks whether its robustness metric is trustworthy or replicates a finding. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022federatedoran} and Yasin et al.~\cite{yasin2025dpfl} are the closest precedents for periodic synchronisation and for federated learning with differential privacy, but both secure the controller rather than characterise a control policy's own privacy-utility trade-off. Gong et al.~\cite{gong2026femaddpg} combine federated training with multi-agent control, two of the three pillars. Like every work above, each of these evaluates its own outcome metric once, on one seed or run.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:system-model}
This section formulates slice admission across a gNB cluster as a decentralised decision problem and describes the two simulators that evaluate it. We consider a cluster of $N$ gNBs, from three in the main campaigns to 37 in the scaling study. Each gNB serves URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC under a shared per-gNB Physical Resource Block (PRB) budget $B$. At each control step every gNB holds zero or more pending requests, each tagged with a slice, and its agent accepts or rejects each one, conditioned on the cluster state. Each decision moves the slice's PRB-ratio ceiling, the control that OpenAirInterface (OAI) exposes to xApps, the external control applications of O-RAN. The binary action matches our single-gNB work, so any coordination benefit comes from the architecture. At three gNBs the cluster is treated as fully connected, a modelling choice, and the scaling study adds ring and hex-grid layouts. The model works at the PRB level and abstracts the radio channel. Channel quality changes how many PRBs a slice needs for the same traffic, and the model folds this into the per-step demand noise. A channel-aware extension that maps the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio to PRB demand is left to future work.

The step reward follows our earlier formulations~\cite{mecon-paper1,icraie-paper2}. It combines a per-slice service term net of SLA-violation penalties with a congestion charge on admitted volume and a load-imbalance penalty. For slice $k$, $\omega_k$ is the priority weight and $R_k$ the per-accept reward. Its admitted count is $n_k$ and its violation penalty $\lambda_k$, and $\mathbf{1}[v_k]$ flags an SLA violation in that step. With these terms, the reward of step $t$ is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t = \sum_{k}\!\big(\omega_k R_k\, n_k - \lambda_k\,\mathbf{1}[v_k]\big)
      \;-\; \kappa\, C_t \lVert \mathbf{a}_t \rVert_1
      \;-\; \beta\,(1 - F_t),
\end{equation}
where $\lVert \mathbf{a}_t \rVert_1$ is the total admitted this step and $C_t$ the cluster-mean congestion level. The congestion coefficient is $\kappa$, and $F_t\!\in\![0,1]$ is the min-over-max PRB-utilisation fairness ratio with load-balance weight $\beta$. The violation flag $v_k$ follows from a continuous SLA margin $m_k$. The correctness-aware metrics of Section~\ref{sec:collapse} also build on this margin. We take it as the worse of the queue and loss channels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin}
m_k = \min\!\Big(1 - \tilde q_k,\; 1 - \tfrac{\ell_k}{\ell_k^{\max}}\Big),
\qquad v_k \Leftrightarrow m_k \le 0,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde q_k$ the slice backlog relative to its budget, and $\ell_k$ and $\ell_k^{\max}$ its loss proxy and loss budget. The weights are set so that, under congestion, the intended policy is differentiated shedding, which keeps accepting URLLC and eMBB and sheds mMTC.

Admission is therefore a decentralised partially observable Markov decision process. At step $t$ the state $s_t$ comprises every gNB's queues, offered demand, and slice ceilings. Agent $i$ observes its own feature row $\mathbf{x}_i$, which the shared encoder combines with the other rows of $\mathbf{X}_t$. For each pending request it chooses $a\in\{\text{accept},\text{reject}\}$, the shared-budget dynamics move the cluster to $s_{t+1}$, and every agent receives the common reward $r_t$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}. The agents jointly seek a policy $\pi$ that maximises the discounted return over an episode of $T{=}60$ steps,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
\max_{\pi}\;\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t} r_t\Big],\qquad \gamma=0.95.
\end{equation}

Each gNB has a budget of $B{=}100$ PRBs in both simulators. The nominal shares are 30, 60, and 10 PRBs for URLLC, eMBB, and mMTC. Each slice's mean offered demand is 105\% of its share, scaled by a fixed per-gNB multiplier $m_i$ between 0.9 and 1.0. This puts a gNB's mean demand at about 95\% to 105\% of its budget, before per-step noise and backlog. When admitted demand exceeds $B$, the slices share it in proportion to demand, so every admitted mMTC request takes resources from URLLC and eMBB. Unserved demand carries forward as backlog within an episode. Env B keeps all of this and adds two mechanisms. Each gNB's mean demand follows $0.95\,(1+0.3\sin(2\pi(s+\psi_i)/60))$ times its nominal shares, again scaled by $m_i$, with $s$ the step and $\psi_i$ a phase redrawn per gNB and episode. As a result, demand swings between about 60\% and 124\% of the budget. A gNB's usable budget also shrinks with its neighbours' previous load, $B_i(t)=B\,(1-0.15\,\bar u_{\mathcal{N}(i)}(t-1))$, a first-order model of inter-cell interference over the same adjacency graph the encoder reads. In Env B shedding every mMTC request leaves URLLC compliance at 62.5\% at three gNBs, whereas a rule that also sheds eMBB when the URLLC queue builds keeps it at 98.9\%. Per-slice compliance counts a slice as compliant only when every gNB meets it, which saturates in Env B at nineteen gNBs, so there we also report per-gNB compliance. \section{The AXIOM Architecture}
\label{sec:methodology}
This section presents the AXIOM architecture, its training rule, and its federated variant. AXIOM differs from its DQN baselines in what each policy sees and shares. AXIOM trains one encoder over the whole cluster from every agent's experience and reads out one Q-head per slice. DQN-SA is one controller over a flattened joint state, and DQN-I gives each gNB its own learner over local features only. The two baselines are therefore ablations that remove the shared encoder.

\caption{AXIOM architecture. a, A shared graph-attention encoder produces one embedding per gNB, and per-slice Q-heads read that gNB's embedding and raw features. b, The federated variant, each gNB a client synchronised by periodic federated averaging with optional privacy noise}

\subsection{Shared Encoder, Decentralised Q-Heads}
\label{sec:methodology-perslice}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}a shows the AXIOM architecture and its data flow. At every step each gNB forms a feature row $\mathbf{x}_i$ of its PRB-used ratio, congestion level, and normalised queue length, and the rows stack into $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$. A two-layer, four-head GAT over the adjacency graph maps $\mathbf{X}$ to one normalised embedding $\mathbf{z}_i$ per gNB, and Section S4 of Online Resource 1 (OR1) gives its attention equations. Every agent's loss updates this encoder, which is centralised training, while each agent decides its own pending requests from its own row $\mathbf{z}_i$, which is decentralised execution.

Two design choices in AXIOM matter most at scale. One small MLP per slice gives each slice its own parameters, so URLLC's large priority weight cannot swamp mMTC's smaller learning signal in a shared head, which Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} confirms at nineteen gNBs. Each node's raw features are also concatenated onto its normalised embedding before the heads, $Q_{\phi_k}([\mathbf{z}_i,\mathbf{x}_i])$, so a head sees absolute queue and congestion levels. From here on, $\mathbf{z}_i$ denotes that concatenated input to the heads. This skip connection and a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, both chosen on Env B pilot seeds before any reported run, are the only changes from the original AXIOM design.

In training, every pending request at gNB $i$, slice $k$ is its own temporal-difference sample, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:gat-ctde} gives the full training loop. For a request with immediate reward $r$ and next-state embedding $\mathbf{z}_i'$, the target $y$ at discount $\gamma$ and the minibatch loss are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td}
y = r + \gamma\,(1-\eta)\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}\!\big(\mathbf{z}_i'\big)[a'],
\qquad
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{(i,k,a)\in \mathcal{M}}\ell_\delta\!\Big(Q_{\phi_k}(\mathbf{z}_i)[a]-y\Big),
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\phi_k^-}$ is slice $k$'s target Q-head, $\eta\in\{0,1\}$ the flag that marks an episode's last step, $\mathcal{M}$ the minibatch of requests, and $\ell_\delta$ the Huber loss. Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} shows that a summed per-step target works as well, so the per-request scheme is a simplification rather than a source of AXIOM's advantage.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Centralised training and decentralised execution of AXIOM}
\label{alg:gat-ctde}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE encoder $f_\theta$, per-slice Q-heads $\{Q_{\phi_k}\}$, targets $f_{\theta^-},\{Q_{\phi_k^-}\}$, buffer $\mathcal{D}$, adjacency $\mathbf{A}$, discount $\gamma$, sync period $T_{\mathrm{s}}$
\FOR{each step $t$, observing joint node features $\mathbf{X}_t$}
  \STATE $\mathbf{E}_t \gets f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_t, \mathbf{A})$ \COMMENT{centralised, one row per gNB}
  \FORALL{pending requests at gNB $i$, slice $k$}
    \STATE $a \gets \arg\max_a Q_{\phi_k}([\mathbf{E}_t[i], \mathbf{X}_t[i]])[a]$, $\epsilon$-greedy \COMMENT{decentralised, own row only}
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE step env, store $(\mathbf{X}_t, \mathbf{X}_{t+1}, \{(i,k,a)\}, r_t, \eta_t)$ in $\mathcal{D}$
  \IF{$|\mathcal{D}| \geq$ warm-up}
    \STATE sample minibatch $\mathcal{M}$, then $\mathbf{E}_{\mathcal{M}} \gets f_\theta(\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{A})$, $\mathbf{E}'_{\mathcal{M}} \gets f_{\theta^-}(\mathbf{X}'_{\mathcal{M}},\mathbf{A})$
    \STATE per request, $y \gets r + \gamma(1{-}\eta)\max_{a'} Q_{\phi_k^-}([\mathbf{E}'_{\mathcal{M}}[i], \mathbf{X}'_{\mathcal{M}}[i]])[a']$
    \STATE $\mathcal{L} \gets$ mean per-request Huber loss, never summed per step, then update $\theta,\{\phi_k\}$ with gradient norm clipped at 1.0
  \ENDIF
  \STATE every $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ episodes, $\theta^-\gets\theta$, $\phi_k^-\gets\phi_k$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Federated Training with Differential Privacy}
Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}b shows the federated variant, in which each gNB holds a private copy of the network, trains only on its own requests, and every $N_{\mathrm{loc}}$ local steps sends its weights for federated averaging (FedAvg)~\cite{lim2020flmec}. Each client can add differential privacy through a differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) step~\cite{dpsgd}, which clips its minibatch gradient and adds Gaussian noise of multiplier $\sigma$. The privacy loss is accounted for with zero-concentrated differential privacy~\cite{zcdp}. Section S4 of OR1 gives the clipping, the privacy accounting, and its assumptions.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
This section describes the campaigns, the baselines, and the statistical tests, followed by the safeguards that protect every reported result. Every campaign uses 300 training and 50 evaluation episodes per seed, where a seed fixes one network initialisation and one environment stream, and each seed is evaluated on a held-out stream. The three-gNB campaigns use 30 seeds, 900 to 929, with a disjoint replication sample, 1000 to 1009. The scaling campaigns use 15 seeds per layout at seven gNBs, 30 at nineteen, and at thirty-seven 30 on the hex layout and 15 on each other layout. MAPPO~\cite{yu2022mappo} is a published multi-agent baseline with a shared actor and a centralised critic, trained on-policy for the same 300 episodes on the same reward. Its learning rate, epochs, and minibatch size were tuned on pilot seeds, because untuned it collapsed in four of five pilot seeds.

Rules need no learning, and accept-all simply admits every request. The oracle rule of Env A sheds mMTC whenever its gNB's congestion level reaches a threshold $\tau$, chosen on training streams only. The selected value is $\tau{=}0$, so the rule sheds every mMTC request. We call it the oracle rule because it is the best rule of its family on training streams. It is a strong reference rather than a proven optimum, since across our Env A samples learned policies exceeded it in 28 of 450 seed-level evaluations, by at most 0.40 reward per step. In Env B we add two tuned rule families, each chosen on training streams over a grid of thresholds and evaluated on the held-out streams. The local family sheds mMTC on the gNB's own congestion or priority-slice queue and sheds eMBB on its URLLC queue, while the neighbour-aware family also reads the mean congestion of the gNB's neighbours. Two further runs repeat the DQN baselines at a learning rate of $10^{-4}$.

All learned policies share $\gamma=0.95$, the training budget, and the exploration schedule of Table~\ref{tab:params}, and Section S4 of OR1 gives the design of the sensitivity studies.

All confidence intervals (CIs) are 95\% bootstrap percentile intervals over 10{,}000 resamples. Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and each family of comparisons against AXIOM is Holm-corrected for multiple testing. The test drops exactly tied pairs, so where two policies make identical decisions on many seeds, as AXIOM and the oracle rule do in Env A, a comparison rests on the remaining seeds.

The settings, seeds, and four hypotheses of the final campaign were fixed in a pre-registration committed to the project's version-control history before any of its runs, rather than lodged with an external registry. Every later campaign was pre-registered the same way. The Env A baselines that this campaign reuses come from an earlier rerun whose plan was committed one minute after that rerun was launched and before any of its results were read. Section S4 of OR1 lists the reused results. H1 is that AXIOM beats both trivial rules in Env B, and H2 compares it with every learned baseline and tuned rule in Env B. H3 asks whether graph attention beats a per-node MLP encoder in Env B at seven gNBs, and H4 compares AXIOM with every baseline at nineteen gNBs in Env A.

A pre-registered validation screen checks every trained checkpoint on its own training stream before evaluation. Every seed is reported whether or not it passes, and Section S4 of OR1 gives the screen's criteria.

\subsection{Evaluation Safeguards}
\label{sec:collapse}
Three safeguards protect the results, and Table~\ref{tab:safeguards} lists the failure each one caught. A trivial-policy check evaluates accept-all and one-slice shedding before any learned policy is trusted, and it exposed an earlier simulator in which accepting every request was optimal.

\label{sec:correctness-metrics}
Correctness-aware metrics form the second safeguard of the evaluation. Block precision $\pi = b_{\text{mMTC}}/b$ is the share of a policy's $b$ blocked requests that target mMTC, and a seed that never blocks counts as a collapse rather than a success. Per-slice SLA compliance then shows whether shedding protects URLLC and eMBB, whereas all-slice compliance scores a correct policy and a failed one alike, because correct shedding gives up mMTC's SLA by design.

Finally, every headline comparison is recomputed on an independent, disjoint seed sample. This caught queue backlog carried over between episodes, and every result here comes from the corrected simulator, which empties all queues at the start of each episode.

Accepting every request is the natural floor at scale. It keeps 70.9\%, 67.1\%, and 61.9\% priority-slice compliance at seven, nineteen, and thirty-seven gNBs. The static rule that sheds every mMTC request keeps 99.96\% to 99.99\% at every size. Where that rule is known, AXIOM comes within 3 points of it up to nineteen gNBs and within 6.3 at thirty-seven, while from seven gNBs up every other learner falls at least 24 points short. Tuning the DQN baselines on pilot seeds does not close the gap, and AXIOM beats each tuned baseline on compliance and on reward at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$, by 29.9 to 48.1 points. The published value-decomposition network (VDN)~\cite{sunehag2018vdn} ties AXIOM at three gNBs but trails it by 13.6 points at nineteen, as Section S6 of OR1 reports.

Fig.~\ref{fig:gnb-map} shows where the gap comes from, gNB by gNB, in the thirty-seven-gNB hex cluster. A gNB's admission gate for a slice is the PRB-ratio ceiling that its accept and reject decisions move between the slice's floor and cap, averaged over every evaluation step of all 30 seeds. AXIOM keeps the URLLC and eMBB gates open at every gNB, at 1.00 and 0.98, and closes the mMTC gate to 0.16 even with its three collapsed seeds included. No baseline closes the mMTC gate below 0.51.

\subsection{Ablations and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results-ablation}
Four checks test where AXIOM's behaviour comes from, as Section S5 of OR1 details. First, the per-slice Q-heads matter as the cluster grows. A variant with one shared head of matched size ties AXIOM at three gNBs. At nineteen gNBs on the ring layout, however, it collapses in 9 of 15 seeds and keeps only 65.9\% priority-slice compliance against AXIOM's 96.8\% on the same seeds, losing every seed at Holm-adjusted $p<0.001$. Second, graph attention adds nothing measurable to AXIOM. A per-node MLP encoder ties AXIOM in Env A. In Env B at seven gNBs it keeps 1.12 and 0.95 more reward per step on the ring and hex layouts and wins 10 of 15 seeds in each. These leads in the pre-registered test H3 miss significance after Holm correction, at $p=0.061$ and $p=0.095$. Third, a variant with one joint summed target in place of per-request samples is never significantly different. Fourth, the learning rate matters, and AXIOM stays within 0.13 of the oracle rule's reward under changed load and slice weights. A 24-configuration MAPPO grid and a seeded DQN-SA still lose to AXIOM in every setting tested.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Fresh-Seed Verification}
\label{sec:results-verify}
We checked the results in three ways, with every pass criterion fixed before the run it judged. First, rerunning eleven reported settings in fresh processes reproduced every stored result exactly. Sixteen further reruns, from seven to thirty-seven gNBs in both environments, also matched exactly. The one exception is DQN-SA, which builds its network before setting the seed, and seeding it leaves AXIOM's leads unchanged.

Second, four further runs retrained every policy on fresh seeds, 10 to 40 per setting, and 81 of their 90 pre-stated checks held. The nine checks that failed concern secondary margins rather than any headline result. On fresh seeds the DQN baselines reached up to 83.3\% compliance at seven gNBs, above the 80\% bound we had set, and AXIOM's small lead over DQN-SA in Env A at three gNBs did not recur. A fifth fresh-seed run held all three of its checks at thirty-seven gNBs on 20 seeds. A sixth held all four in Env B at nineteen gNBs on 15 seeds, as Section S7 of OR1 reports. Section S2 of OR1 compares each reported difference from a learned baseline with its fresh-seed counterpart, and 35 of the 38 whose CI excludes zero hold.

Third, a script written independently of the analysis pipeline, with its own log reader and Holm correction, recomputes the numbers in every results table and the key results sentences from the raw logs. In the current version, all 620 of its checks pass.

\subsection{Federation, Privacy, Disruption, and Live Deployment}
\label{sec:results-m3}
\label{sec:results-m4}
\label{sec:results-m8}
Federated training costs AXIOM little reward per step on average. Pooled over all 30 federated seeds, ten of them fresh, the cost is $+0.491$ per step with 95\% CI [$-$0.08, 1.09] and $p=0.14$. Even so, a cost of up to about one reward per step cannot be excluded. Per-step DP-SGD noise lowers reward by 0.77 to 1.25 per step without a significant drop, but its formal guarantee is weak, with $\varepsilon$ of about 2{,}600 or more. Under gNB dropout and agent churn every policy loses reward significantly, and no gap between AXIOM and any baseline survives Holm correction at ten seeds. Demand spikes leave AXIOM's decisions largely intact, with block precision at 0.98, and Section S5 of OR1 gives the protocol. A live single-gNB OAI testbed, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:testbed}, confirmed the control path that AXIOM would use, with 0.000\% loss at one and at two UEs per slice, but it never ran short of capacity, as Section S3 of OR1 reports.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
No learned baseline earns significantly more reward than AXIOM in any setting we tested, and its margin over the cheaper MAPPO is largest under time-varying load, coupling, and uneven per-gNB load. A rule known in advance, however, matches or beats AXIOM in steady conditions at every size, and AXIOM beats a tuned rule only when the load moves beyond the rule's tuning conditions. Its practical case is for operators who cannot hand-tune and re-tune rules as conditions change, rather than for a fixed, well-understood cluster.

Several limitations bound the findings of this paper. First, both of our simulators are synthetic rather than measured. They use a first-order coupling model with no radio channel, user mobility, or measured traffic traces, and the live testbed never reached contention. External validity on hardware under load therefore remains the main open question. Second, we did not reimplement a published slice-admission algorithm, so our learned baselines are standard multi-agent methods and ablations of AXIOM rather than prior admission controllers. Third, attention added nothing over a per-node encoder, and a value-decomposition baseline matched AXIOM at three gNBs, so AXIOM's contribution is a design that scales rather than a new learning principle. Fourth, charging the SLA penalty per violating gNB, so that it grows with the cluster, changed neither AXIOM's compliance nor its standing against the static rule. Finally, the pre-registrations were committed to the authors' own version-control history rather than an external registry, and development work that changed no reported result is disclosed in Section S4 of OR1.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper presented AXIOM, a multi-agent slice admission controller with a shared graph-attention encoder and per-slice heads, evaluated under a protocol designed to make its results trustworthy. AXIOM's advantage over learned controllers grows with the cluster. Under static load it reaches 98.9\% of an oracle rule's reward at three gNBs and keeps priority-slice compliance between 93.7\% and 97.3\% from seven to thirty-seven gNBs, while every learned baseline stays below 84\%. At thirty-seven gNBs its lead over the best learned baseline reaches 35.0 points, and 36.5 points on 20 fresh seeds, with 7,782 parameters against up to 682,058.

Under time-varying load and coupling AXIOM earns 27\% to 36\% more reward than MAPPO at three and nineteen gNBs, so its extra cost pays most where the environment is dynamic. When load rises 10\% to 20\% above the conditions a tuned rule was set for, AXIOM overtakes that rule at nineteen gNBs by 6.2 to 8.7 reward per step without retraining. Simple rules still set the bar in other settings. A static rule known in advance does better under steady load and at nineteen gNBs under coupling, and a tuned rule beats every learned policy at its tuning conditions, as Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses. The main findings held on fresh seeds never used before.

More broadly, the evaluation safeguards show that learned admission control should be judged against trivial and tuned rules. Its metrics should be tied to the objective, and its results should hold on independent seeds under a pre-registered analysis.

Three directions for future work follow from these limits. First, a live multi-gNB deployment of AXIOM would test its gains under real contention. Second, giving the learner the queue-threshold structure that the tuned rules exploit may close the remaining gap to them. Finally, adding noise to the per-round model updates rather than to every step would make the formal privacy guarantee meaningful.
